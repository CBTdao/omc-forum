Capex is a definition: the $207B quarter three of four hyperscalers could not pay for (2026)

In the September 2026 quarter, Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta put about $207 billion into capital projects on their own definitions — and three of the four generated less operating cash than they spent. Microsoft's guidance fell 8% without a single project being cancelled, because data-centre buildings quietly went from 15-year to 25-year lives. The build is now funded by debt, leases and inventory, and each of those sits in a different line of the accounts.

DCF EditorialNov 23, 202613 min read
TL;DR

The capex number is a definition before it is a measurement. In Q3 2026 (calendar), Alphabet spent $51.4B on property and equipment, Amazon $75.9B net of proceeds, Microsoft about $45B including finance leases, and Meta $34.9B including finance-lease principal — roughly $207B combined, on four slightly different definitions (our sum). Three of the four burned cash: Alphabet's free cash flow was −$9.1B (its second straight negative quarter), Meta's −$2.1B, and Amazon's trailing-twelve-month free cash flow −$42.4B against +$14.8B a year earlier. Only Microsoft stayed positive at +$18.6B — and its calendar-2026 capex guidance was cut from about $190B to about $175B for an accounting reason, not an investment one: from 1 July 2026 it extended data-centre building lives from 15 to 25 years, which pushed more new leases into operating treatment and out of the capex line. The money came from somewhere: long-term debt at Alphabet, Amazon and Meta rose from $170.9B to $379.1B in nine months (+$208B, our arithmetic from the balance sheets). And Alphabet's inventory went from $2.4B to $13.3B because TPU systems are built before they are billed — capex turned into stock, and most of the revenue lands in 2027.

Why this post exists: three weeks ago we traced how the four hyperscalers extended the useful lives of their servers and what that did to earnings. This post is the other half of the same accounting story, one level down: what happens when the capital-spending line itself stops measuring spending. The data comes from the Q3 2026 earnings 8-K exhibits filed with the SEC — Alphabet's exhibit 99.1 dated 2026-10-28, Amazon's dated 2026-10-29, Meta's dated 2026-10-21 and Microsoft's fiscal-Q1 filing dated 2026-10-28 — and every aggregate below is cross-checked against three independent capex trackers that compile the same filings. Where a number is our arithmetic on official inputs, it says so.

How much did the four hyperscalers actually spend in Q3 2026?

Here is the quarter, on each company's own definition. The definitions are not the same, and that is the first finding rather than a footnote: Microsoft and Meta include finance leases, Amazon reports purchases of property and equipment net of proceeds and incentives, and Alphabet's cash capex has no lease component at all. Adding the four rows produces a number that is close to a total, not a total.

Sum the rows and you get roughly $207B in one quarter (ours). For scale, Amazon alone spent $175.7B gross on property and equipment in the first nine months of 2026, against $92.3B in the same period of 2025 — a 90% increase in nine months, disclosed in its own cash-flow statement. Alphabet spent $132.0B in nine months against $63.6B a year earlier, a 107% increase. These are not rounding-error differences between accounting policies; they are the largest private capital programmes in industrial history, and they are being run through four different definitions of the word "capex".

Why did Microsoft's capex number fall when its spending did not?

This is the cleanest example of the definition problem, and it was disclosed almost in passing. On its June-quarter earnings call, Microsoft's CFO Amy Hood said the company would extend the estimated useful life of its data-centre and office buildings from 15 years to 25 years, effective 1 July 2026 — the start of its 2027 fiscal year. Because a longer assumed life changes how a lease is classified, Hood said the change "will cause more of our data-centre leases to be classified as operating leases rather than finance leases." Finance leases count in Microsoft's disclosed capex; operating leases do not. The guidance consequence: calendar-2026 capex guidance moved from about $190B to about $175B, a cut of roughly 8%, while Hood stated that the company's actual investment expectation was unchanged.

Read that again as an accountant would. The same buildings, the same orders, the same power contracts — a different assumed life, and $15B of 2026 capex vanishes from the reported figure. It does not vanish from the world. It reappears as operating-lease payments inside operating cash flow, which is exactly where a reader looking at "capex" will not look. Note also the second-order effect that runs the opposite way from the server-life story: extending a building's life lowers annual depreciation, which flatters operating income, while reclassifying leases raises operating expense, which does not. Two effects, opposite signs, one disclosure sentence.

The operational data confirms nothing slowed down. Microsoft's fiscal Q4 2026 capex was about $41B, up more than 70% year on year, with roughly two-thirds going to short-lived assets such as CPUs and GPUs. Its fiscal Q1 2027 operating cash flow was $58.0B and cash capex $39.4B — and it still came in under its own "more than $50B" quarterly guide because, per Hood on the call as reported by CNBC, some third-party capacity deliveries arrived late. A company that is delaying delivery of rented GPUs is not a company that has stopped building.

Why did free cash flow go negative at three of the four?

Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure, and in Q3 2026 that subtraction went below zero at Alphabet, Meta and Amazon. Alphabet's operating cash flow of $42.3B fell $9.1B short of its $51.4B capex — its second consecutive negative quarter after −$5.9B in Q2, even though its trailing-twelve-month free cash flow is still +$19.7B because the first half of the year was positive. Meta's operating cash flow of $32.8B fell $2.1B short of capex including lease principal. Amazon is the extreme case on a trailing basis: −$42.4B of free cash flow over twelve months, against +$14.8B a year earlier, which Amazon attributes directly to a $94.8B year-over-year increase in property and equipment purchases.

The only positive number is Microsoft's +$18.6B, and it is worth being precise about why. Microsoft's operating cash flow of $58.0B is enormous relative to its $39.4B of cash capex, and part of the gap is structural: Microsoft rents a larger share of its capacity from third parties than its peers build, which lowers capex and raises operating expense. That is a legitimate strategy, and it is also why its capex line is the least comparable of the four. The lesson is not "Microsoft is disciplined and the others are not". The lesson is that the free-cash-flow sign is a function of how much of the build sits on your own balance sheet — and that choice is now visible in a single quarter's numbers.

Where is the money coming from?

When capex exceeds operating cash flow, the difference has to be financed, and the 2026 filings show the financing arriving in the least glamorous line of the balance sheet. Amazon's long-term debt went from $65.6B at 31 December 2025 to $173.1B at 30 September 2026, with $115.1B of long-term debt proceeds in the first nine months against $0.7B in the same period a year earlier. Alphabet's long-term debt went from $46.5B to $125.1B. Meta's went from $58.7B to $80.9B, with $24.9B of net proceeds in nine months. Added together, the three borrowers took on roughly $208B of new long-term debt in three quarters (ours, from the balance sheets).

Debt is not the only channel. Leases move the obligation off the capex line and onto a schedule of payments; Microsoft's operating-lease cost rose from $5.5B to $7.0B in fiscal 2026 and finance-lease amortisation from $3.4B to $5.4B, both disclosed in its annual report. Inventory is a third channel, and it is the one specific to this cycle. Equity is a fourth: Alphabet raised $12.4B through an at-the-market programme in the quarter, per its filing as tracked by two independent capex trackers — a company with a $4T market cap selling stock to fund servers.

The strategic reading is uncomfortable for the "AI capex is a bubble" and the "AI capex is fine" camps alike. If the spending were discretionary, a negative free-cash-flow quarter would be the moment to slow down; instead all four guided higher for 2027, with Amazon saying "meaningfully more", Alphabet "increase significantly" and Meta continuing to "invest aggressively". Borrowing to fund an investment with a multi-year payback is a bet on the cost of capital staying below the return on the compute. The debt issuance is the bet, priced.

Why does Alphabet's inventory line matter more than its capex line?

Because Alphabet started selling TPU systems as complete systems into customers' data centres, and that business has a cash-flow shape nobody associates with a cloud provider. Alphabet's own earnings release says "TPU systems sales were also a meaningful contributor to revenue growth" in Q3. Its balance sheet shows inventory going from $2.4B to $13.3B in nine months, and its cash-flow statement shows inventory consuming $11.0B of cash in that period against $1.2B a year earlier — a 9.5× increase in the working-capital cost of the business.

The mechanism is simple and brutal: when you sell a rack of accelerators, you build the hardware before you can bill for it. The capex goes out at the start; the cash comes in at delivery and acceptance, and on Alphabet's own call its CFO said that only a small fraction of contracted TPU revenue is recognised in 2026, with the substantial majority arriving in 2027. So the same dollar of investment is counted twice against cash flow — once as capex when the plant and equipment is bought, once as inventory when the finished system waits to ship — and only then becomes revenue. That is why Alphabet can report 103% Google Cloud growth to $30.7B with $11.1B of cloud operating income in the same quarter it reports negative free cash flow. The income statement and the cash-flow statement are describing two different years.

What does Amazon's guidance arithmetic imply for Q4?

Amazon kept its 2026 cash capex guidance at about $220B while spending $75.9B in a single quarter. Run the subtraction: with $175.7B of gross purchases already booked in nine months, the full-year guide leaves roughly $44B for the fourth quarter — a step down of about 43% from Q3's pace. On the net basis Amazon guides on, the nine-month figure is about $172.2B, leaving roughly $48B, still a fall of about 37% (ours). Either Amazon pulled spending forward into Q3 and will genuinely slow in Q4, or the annual figure is stale and will be raised in February. Management has already said 2027 will be "meaningfully more", which makes the second explanation the more likely one — and makes the $220B a floor rather than a forecast.

Contrast Alphabet, whose guidance points the other way: $132.0B spent in nine months against a $195–205B full-year guide implies $63–73B in Q4, a step up from Q3's $51.4B. Two companies with the same headline story — "AI capex keeps rising" — and opposite quarterly implications, because one guided on a number it has already nearly reached and the other guided on a number it has already passed. Anyone tracking "hyperscaler capex" as a single series is averaging these two shapes into noise.

What should you watch next quarter?

Three lines, in order of information value. First, operating-lease additions — the place where the build now hides when it is not in capex. Microsoft's operating-lease additions are disclosed annually, and any peer that follows the same reclassification will show the same pattern: flat capex, rising lease payments, rising operating expense. Second, inventory — for Alphabet specifically, because it is the only one of the four converting capex into stock at scale; if inventory keeps growing faster than revenue, the TPU business is building ahead of demand rather than to it. Third, the gap between guided capex and the implied quarterly run-rate — Amazon's $220B implies a Q4 that contradicts Q3, and one of those two numbers will be revised. A capex guide that is not consistent with the last quarter's spend is not a forecast; it is a placeholder.

And keep the definitions attached to every number you repeat. "Microsoft cut capex 8%" is true and misleading; "Microsoft changed an accounting estimate and the spending did not move" is the sentence that survives contact with the filing. In a cycle where four companies spend $200B a quarter, the difference between those two sentences is worth about $15B.

Key takeaways
  • The four numbers are not the same measurement. Microsoft and Meta include finance leases, Amazon reports net of proceeds, Alphabet excludes leases — the ~$207B total is our sum of four definitions, and the definition is where the story is.
  • Microsoft's 8% capex cut was accounting, not restraint. Building lives went from 15 to 25 years on 1 July 2026, pushing more data-centre leases into operating treatment and out of the capex line; the CFO said the investment expectation was unchanged.
  • Three of four burned cash. Alphabet −$9.1B (second straight quarter), Meta −$2.1B, Amazon −$42.4B on a trailing basis; only Microsoft stayed positive at +$18.6B, partly because it rents more capacity than it builds.
  • The funding is debt. Long-term debt at Alphabet, Amazon and Meta rose from $170.9B to $379.1B in nine months — about $208B of new borrowing to cover a cash gap that capex guidance says will widen.
  • Alphabet's inventory is capex in transit. $2.4B → $13.3B in nine months because TPU systems are built before they are billed, which is why 103% cloud growth and negative free cash flow can both be true.
  • Watch operating-lease additions and the guide-versus-run-rate gap. Amazon's $220B implies a Q4 step down of a third from Q3's pace — either spending was pulled forward or the guide gets raised in February.
