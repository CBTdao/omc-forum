Half of GE Vernova's backlog isn't machines — it's decades of labor (2026)

When the turbine queue makes headlines, the services book gets a footnote. It shouldn't: GE Vernova's Q3 2026 backlog of $197 billion — which the company's own press release defines as remaining performance obligation, the same accounting line Micron used for its memory contracts — splits into $104 billion of equipment (up 92% year over year) and $93 billion of services (up 14%). Nearly half the order book is not turbines. It is twenty-year maintenance agreements attached to turbines already in the ground.

DCF EditorialNov 16, 202612 min read
TL;DR

The gas turbine shortage story has a quieter twin: the service-annuity story, and the annuity is now 47% of the backlog. GE Vernova's third quarter 2026 (reported October 28, 2026) by its own press release: orders of $29 billion, up 96% organically; backlog growth of $20 billion sequentially to $197 billion, with management guiding it above $200 billion in Q4; gas Power equipment backlog and slot reservation agreements up from 116 to 122 GW, expected to reach at least 130 GW by year-end; revenue $12.6 billion, up 27%; adjusted EBITDA $1.9 billion at a 15.4% margin, up 690 basis points organically; free cash flow $5.4 billion. On the earnings call (as transcribed by two independent outlets), CFO Ken Parks split the backlog: equipment $104 billion, up ~$16 billion sequentially and 92% year over year (including the acquired Prolec backlog), services ~$11 billion higher, up 14% year over year, to $93 billion, led by Power. The reversal sits in the growth rates: services backlog grew 14% while services revenue grew only 6% — the contracted annuity is compounding faster than it converts, because the 32 gas turbines shipped this quarter (up 60%) are future service contracts being installed, not just machines being sold (analysis, ours).

The question this piece answers: when an equipment maker's backlog doubles in a year, what exactly did it sell — and which half of the sale repeats? Every number below comes from GE Vernova's own Q3 2026 press release (October 28, 2026, gevernova.com) or from its earnings call as transcribed by two independent outlets (Yahoo Finance and Benzinga, whose figures agree line for line). Where we do arithmetic, it is marked. Nothing here involves crypto assets; this is power equipment accounting.

What does the $197 billion backlog actually contain?

First, the definition, because it does more work than it looks like. The press release's own footnote: backlog is "defined as remaining performance obligation (RPO)" — the accounting term for contracted revenue not yet recognized. That is the same line item that carried Micron's ~$150 billion of take-or-pay memory obligations last week. It is not a pipeline of hopes; it is signed work the company has not yet delivered or performed.

The split, per the CFO on the call:

Two ratios fall out immediately (ours). Services are 47.2% of the total backlog — a nearly even split between "build the machine" and "keep the machine running." And the whole RPO equals roughly 3.9 years of current-quarter revenue annualized ($197B ÷ $50.4B). A customer signing with GE Vernova today is booking delivery windows and a maintenance relationship that stretch most of the next decade — which is the same queue-length arithmetic as the slot calendar we documented earlier this month, now visible on the balance sheet.

Why do the two books grow at different speeds?

Because they convert on different clocks, and the quarter's revenue line shows it. Equipment revenue rose 26% year over year (Power +65%, Electrification +21%, partially offset by lower Wind revenue). Services revenue rose 6%, with growth in Onshore Wind and Power and positive price. The equipment book is enormous and fast-converting — a 92% backlog jump feeds revenue within quarters, since gas turbines ship (32 this quarter, including 13 aeroderivatives, up 60% year over year) and recognize. The services book is smaller in flow and slow in conversion: a twenty-year service agreement recognizes as the work happens, decade by decade.

Now put the growth rates side by side: services backlog +14% against services revenue +6%. The contracted annuity is growing more than twice as fast as the revenue it produces (ours, reading the two disclosed rates together). That gap is the installed-base effect, and the CFO said it plainly: "the growth in our installed base will continue to generate strong services growth at high margins." Each turbine shipped enlarges the fleet that service agreements bill against for decades. The 32 turbines delivered this quarter are not just this quarter's revenue — they are 32 multi-year service relationships being commissioned.

There is a rough cross-industry check on the services number (ours, a heuristic rather than a reconciliation). Siemens Energy — the other half of the big-two gas turbine duopoly — has told investors its service agreements run above €400 million per gigawatt over twenty years. At that unit annuity, GE Vernova's 122 GW of gas equipment backlog and slot reservations alone would imply on the order of €49 billion (roughly $53 billion) of lifetime gas-service value — the right order of magnitude next to a $93 billion services book that also spans nuclear, wind and grid services. Two different companies, two different disclosures, one consistent picture: the turbine is priced like a machine and monetizes like a subscription.

Who is financing the backlog?

Follow the cash, because the quarter's cash flow statement answers who is actually paying for the buildout. Free cash flow was $5.4 billion in the quarter, up $4.7 billion year over year — against revenue of $12.6 billion, a 43% quarterly FCF margin (ours). The driver is disclosed: working capital contributed $5.3 billion, "driven primarily by down payments on increased orders and slot reservations at Power, as well as down payments at Electrification" (CFO, per the two transcripts). Cash ended at roughly $17 billion, up $4 billion sequentially, after returning $1.8 billion to shareholders in the quarter and $5.7 billion in the first nine months; the board raised the buyback authorization from $10 billion to $20 billion in October.

Read that against the two financing stories this forum has covered this month, and three business models line up in a row (analysis, ours). The AI server builders (Dell, Supermicro) hold the working capital — they buy the memory and GPUs first and collect later, which is why their gross margins rose while their cash fell. Micron flipped the burden — 26 take-or-pay contracts with $12.7 billion of customer deposits pre-funding its fabs. GE Vernova is the extreme version: customers pay down payments to hold a place in the queue for machines that will ship years from now, and those down payments are so large that they drove most of a $5.4 billion free cash flow quarter. In all three cases the direction is the same — the customer finances the capacity expansion — but GE Vernova collects before it has even built the product. The queue itself is a financed asset.

What does this mean for anyone buying compute?

Three translations. First: the services annuity is the durable pricing power. Equipment backlog margins are "expanding" on favorable price, but a 20-year service agreement written at 2026 rates outlives any equipment price correction. When analysts model a gas turbine glut arriving in the 2030s as new capacity from Siemens, Mitsubishi and others lands, the equipment price cycle can soften while the installed-base service stream keeps billing — that asymmetry is why management calls services the high-margin half. Second: the installed base is the moat, and it is compounding right now. 32 turbines shipped this quarter and 122 GW contracted mean the service book's growth is already scheduled; a buyer negotiating service terms in 2027 negotiates against a bigger installed base than exists today. Third: for data center developers — the marginal ~15% of buyers from our slot-calendar piece — the total cost of a turbine is the machine plus two decades of service attached to it. Anyone comparing GE Vernova against alternatives on equipment price alone is comparing the subscription's signup fee and ignoring the subscription.

And for the home-lab reader: the annuity logic scales all the way down. The vendor that sold you the UPS wants a battery-replacement contract; the vendor that sold you the NAS wants a drive-failure plan. The same rule applies at every scale — the margin lives in the relationship, not the box — and the same defense applies too: know your hold-up times and your transfer times before anyone sells you the service contract (today's Homelab piece).

What are the caveats?

Transcript vs filing. The equipment/services split comes from the earnings call as transcribed by Yahoo Finance and Benzinga — two independent transcriptions agreeing line for line — but it is a call disclosure, not an audited statement; the press release confirms the $197B total, the GW figures and the headline financials. The Prolec inclusion. Equipment backlog "incorporates Prolec backlog," so the +92% year-over-year figure mixes organic growth with an acquisition; the sequential +$16B is the cleaner growth read. The per-GW cross-check is a heuristic. Siemens Energy's >€400M/GW service annuity is a different company's disclosure applied to GE Vernova's GW base; it is an order-of-magnitude check, clearly labeled, not a reconciliation. Backlog is not revenue. RPO converts over decades and carries execution risk — the press release's own risk factors include "our ability to execute and estimate long-term service obligations." And nothing here is investment advice; it is a reading of public filings because the same contract structures keep deciding what compute costs.

Key takeaways
  • The backlog is half annuity. $197B total RPO = $104B equipment (+92% YoY, incl. Prolec) + $93B services (+14% YoY, led by Power). Services are 47.2% of the book (ours).
  • Backlog is RPO, not a wish list. GE Vernova's own footnote defines backlog as remaining performance obligation — the same accounting line as Micron's $150B of take-or-pay memory contracts.
  • The annuity compounds faster than it converts. Services backlog +14% YoY vs services revenue +6%; equipment revenue +26% shows the two books converting on different clocks. The gap is the installed-base effect: 32 turbines shipped (+60%) are future service contracts being commissioned.
  • The per-GW check holds. At Siemens Energy's quoted >€400M/GW twenty-year service annuity, GEV's 122 GW of gas alone implies ~€49B (~$53B) of lifetime service value — the right order next to a $93B book spanning more than gas (heuristic, ours).
  • Customers finance the queue. Working capital supplied $5.3B of the quarter's $5.4B FCF — "down payments on increased orders and slot reservations." FCF margin was 43% of revenue for the quarter; cash ~$17B, buyback authorization doubled to $20B.
  • Three models, one direction. AI server builders hold the working capital; Micron's customers deposit against allocation; GE Vernova collects down payments years before delivery. The customer pays for capacity expansion in all three — the OEM just collects earliest.
  • Price the subscription, not the box. A turbine's real cost is the machine plus twenty years of attached service; the margin lives in the relationship at every scale, from grid operators to home racks.
