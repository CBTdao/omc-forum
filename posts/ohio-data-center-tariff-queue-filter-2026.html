Ohio turned a 30,000 MW queue into 5,642 MW of contracts — that was the point (2026)

In May 2024, AEP Ohio faced applications for more than 30,000 MW of new data-center load — roughly three times the largest peak its system had ever carried. By February 2026, after the country's first data-center-specific tariff took effect, the queue had been filtered into 5,642 MW of binding contracts — each backed by collateral, exit fees, and a minimum monthly bill for 85% of contracted capacity whether or not the electrons move. The headline is not "data centers fled Ohio." It is that Ohio built a machine for converting hype into contracts, and the machine worked.

DCF EditorialNov 20, 202612 min read
TL;DR

A state tariff did what no load-forecasting model could: it made speculative demand identify itself by paying. The mechanics: under the data-center tariff adopted by Ohio's Public Utilities Commission (PUCO) on July 9, 2025 and effective July 23, 2025 (PUCO Case No. 24-0508-EL-ATA), new large data centers — over 25 MW, signing electric service agreements after the settlement — must pay for at least 85% of contracted capacity every month even if they use less, for up to 12 years including a 4-year ramp, post collateral (up to 50% of full-term minimum charges if they don't meet credit criteria), and pay exit fees for early departure. The filter's results: applications exceeding 30,000 MW (against an AEP Ohio historic peak of roughly 8,000–10,500 MW) shrank to 13,022.7 MW at the paid-study stage, then to 5,642 MW of binding contracts as of February 12, 2026 — an 80%+ reduction. Those 5,642 MW sit alongside 12,219 MW signed before the tariff, for 17,861 MW of contracted capacity through 2035 — about 1.7× the top of the utility's historic peak range. The rule is spreading: FirstEnergy's Ohio utilities filed their own version in June 2026, Virginia approved a large-load tariff, and an Ohio bill (HB 706) would set statewide minimums. The open risk: the tariff is under appeal at the Supreme Court of Ohio (briefing concluded March 24, 2026; no decision as of July 17, 2026), and its 12-year contracts have never been stress-tested by a downturn.

The question this piece answers: when a data-center queue collapses by 80%, is that a boom ending — or a billing system starting to work? Every number below comes from AEP's own press releases, the Ohio Consumers' Counsel (OCC), PUCO's own statements, and multiple independent secondary reports, cross-checked on 2026-11-20. Where a figure rests on a single compiled source, the piece says so; where it is our own arithmetic, it says that too. This post is deliberately a companion to our earlier take-or-pay piece — that one treated capacity auctions as options; this one treats a state tariff as a queue filter.

What is the AEP Ohio data-center tariff?

It is a rate class: a bundle of regulated prices and conditions that applies to one category of customer — in this case, new large data centers. The case began in May 2024, when AEP Ohio (the American Electric Power utility serving about 1.5 million customers across 61 of Ohio's 88 counties) asked the PUCO to reconcile the cost of building infrastructure for explosive data-center growth. The settlement filed on October 23, 2024 — signed not only by the utility but by PUCO staff, the Ohio Consumers' Counsel, the Ohio Energy Group, Ohio Partners for Affordable Energy and Walmart — was adopted by the Commission on July 9, 2025, with the compliance tariff effective July 23, 2025. A second, competing settlement — pushed by a data-center coalition led by Amazon and offering 75% minimum demand, shorter commitments and smaller exit fees — was rejected.

The scope matters as much as the numbers: the tariff applies to new data centers with load above 25 MW that sign electric service agreements after the settlement date. Existing agreements are grandfathered — unless they expand by more than 25 MW over contracted capacity, per contemporaneous coverage of the order. A sliding scale gives small and mid-sized data centers more flexibility.

What exactly does the tariff require?

Four mechanisms, each aimed at a different failure mode of speculative load:

One, a minimum demand charge. Large new data-center customers pay for at least 85% of contracted capacity every month, even if they use less — for up to 12 years, including a 4-year ramp-up period. Per the technical read of the compliance tariff, the initial term is the ramp period plus eight years, and the 85% floor is calculated on contract capacity within rate-class rules. Two, collateral. Customers who do not simultaneously meet specified credit-rating and cash conditions must post security equal to about 50% of the contract's full-term minimum charges. Three, exit fees if a project is canceled or cannot meet its obligations over the term. Four, proof of financial viability before the agreement is signed at all.

Each mechanism prices one specific risk: minimum demand charges price the risk of a half-empty substation; collateral prices abandonment; exit fees price early departure; the viability test prices the queue itself — you cannot reserve a place in line for free.

How big was the queue before the filter?

Data-center developers submitted applications to AEP Ohio totaling more than 30,000 MW. To size that number: AEP Ohio's own historic system peak sits around 8,000–10,500 MW — the applications, taken at face value, were roughly three times everything the utility had ever served at once. None of it was a commitment. Some applicants would never secure financing, land, permits or tenants; applications are free, and free options get exercised.

This is the failure mode the tariff attacks. A utility that builds transmission against face-value applications either overbuilds (and every other customer pays) or underbuilds (and the real projects wait years). AEP's own filing in February 2026 states the tariff's purpose in one line, as quoted in industry coverage: the procedure's primary purpose is "to eliminate speculative and uncommitted data center load" from planning.

How much of the queue survived the filter?

The collapse came in two gates, and the arithmetic of both is public. Gate one — the paid study. After the preliminary screening stage, developers requested grid studies for 13,022.7 MW — less than half the face-value queue, and the first real money spent. Gate two — the binding contract. By February 12, 2026, capacity signed under the new tariff into binding agreements was 5,642 MW — an 80%+ reduction from the application peak, and just over 19% of the original 30,000 MW (our arithmetic). PUCO Chair Jenifer French put the filter's result plainly, in comments quoted in trade coverage: "While 5,642 MW is still a lot of load, it's a lot less than 30,000 MW. This tariff unquestionably helped AEP Ohio to refine the amount of load in its data center queue, thereby saving other ratepayers millions of dollars in unnecessary infrastructure."

Two adjustments keep the picture honest. First, the tariff-era contracts sit alongside a legacy book: 12,219 MW of data-center capacity signed before the tariff took effect. Total contracted projects reached 17,861 MW, scheduled to phase in through 2035 — not simultaneously. Second, that surviving book is still enormous relative to the system that must serve it: 17,861 MW is about 1.7× the top of AEP Ohio's 8,000–10,500 MW historic peak range (our arithmetic, and one that several independent write-ups converge on). The filter did not shrink the buildout; it separated the part of the buildout that has money attached from the part that only had press releases attached.

Why is a shrinking queue the success case?

Because the thing that shrank was never real demand — it was the price of pretending. Before the tariff, an application was a free call option on grid capacity: the utility absorbed the cost of studying, planning and (if the option cluster looked real) building, while the applicant risked nothing. After the tariff, the same option costs an 85% minimum bill for up to 12 years plus collateral plus an exit fee. Options that are worthless at that price were never demand. We think the right way to read the 30,000→5,642 collapse is as the first large-scale revelation of true demand under a binding price — the load-growth equivalent of a book building exercise where the book finally has money behind it.

The second reversal is about what this does to financing. A 12-year take-or-pay stream with collateral and exit fees is not a tariff; it is credit enhancement. Transmission capex backed by contracted, financially committed load can be financed at materially lower risk than capex backed by forecasts — this is the same structural logic that turned LNG's decade of contracting into investable infrastructure, happening in Ohio in six months. That is also why the rule is spreading faster than any model ever did: FirstEnergy's Ohio utilities (Ohio Edison, Cleveland Electric Illuminating, Toledo Edison) filed their own data-center tariff in June 2026 — billing at the greater of actual demand or 85% of contract capacity over a 10-year ramped term (per Brakey Energy's regulatory roundup, a single compiled source, treat accordingly); PUCO staff recommended a data-center class in AES Ohio's pending rate plan; Virginia approved a large-load tariff — bringing AEP's states with approved large-load tariffs to five as of late July 2026 (per AEP); and Ohio's HB 706 would set statewide minimums (still in committee as of mid-July 2026, per the OCC).

What could break it?

Two things, and both are live. First, the courts. The Ohio Manufacturers' Association Energy Group appealed the tariff to the Supreme Court of Ohio in November 2025, arguing it discriminates against energy-intensive users; merits briefing concluded March 24, 2026, and no decision had issued as of July 17, 2026. The OCC — the consumer advocate that co-signed the settlement — joined the case to defend it. An adverse ruling would not just unwind Ohio's tariff; it would ripple through every state copying the template, and through the financing assumptions of every utility that has already re-forecast its load on contracted-only numbers. Second, the first real downturn. The tariff's 12-year commitments have, as Ohio's own state audit coverage notes, never been stress-tested: no binding contract has yet been walked away from in a recession or an AI capex winter. Exit fees are priced for the case they were designed for — but the actual cost of abandoning a half-built 500 MW campus, and who eats it, remains a paper answer.

One honest caveat on interpretation: contracted capacity is not energized load. AEP's disclosure is careful on this point — the 5,642 and 12,219 MW figures are contract commitments, with delivery phased through 2035 and subject to each project actually being built. What the tariff guarantees is not that the electrons flow; it is that someone pays if they don't.

How should a compute buyer read this?

Three habits transfer. First, always ask which load numbers have money attached. "X GW of data-center demand" is meaningless until you know what fraction is binding contracts with minimum bills — in Ohio, the answer went from roughly 0% of the queue to 100% of the queue, and the headline number fell by 80% in the process. Second, treat tariff design as demand disclosure. The states copying Ohio's 85% floor are not protecting ratepayers from data centers so much as forcing developers to reveal which projects are real; the tariff is a screening technology, and screening results are leading indicators of actual construction. Third, watch the appeal docket, not the press releases. The template's national future is a legal question now, and the Supreme Court of Ohio's decision will reprice grid capex risk in every state that has adopted or is considering the model.

Key takeaways
  • The tariff (PUCO Case 24-0508-EL-ATA, adopted 2025-07-09, effective 2025-07-23) requires new large data centers (>25 MW) to pay ≥85% of contracted capacity monthly for up to 12 years, post collateral (~50% of full-term minimum charges if credit criteria aren't met), and pay exit fees.
  • The queue filtered in two gates: 30,000+ MW of applications → 13,022.7 MW of paid studies → 5,642 MW of binding contracts by 2026-02-12 — an 80%+ reduction; only ~19% of the speculative queue converted (our arithmetic).
  • The surviving book is still enormous: 12,219 MW pre-tariff + 5,642 MW under the tariff = 17,861 MW through 2035 — about 1.7× the top of AEP Ohio's 8,000–10,500 MW historic peak range.
  • The reversal: a shrinking queue is the success case — AEP's own filing says the tariff's purpose is eliminating speculative load, and PUCO Chair French credited it with saving ratepayers "millions of dollars in unnecessary infrastructure."
  • Minimum demand charges are credit enhancement — contracted load makes transmission capex financeable, which is why the model is spreading: FirstEnergy filed its own version (June 2026), Virginia approved a large-load tariff (AEP states with one: five), Ohio HB 706 would set statewide minimums.
  • Two live risks: the Supreme Court of Ohio appeal (filed Nov 2025, briefed 2026-03-24, undecided as of 2026-07-17) and zero stress tests — no 12-year contract has yet been abandoned in a downturn.
