The packaging wall: CoWoS at 5.5x reticle, and the film under every AI chip (2026)

The bottleneck story has already moved twice: from GPUs to power, then from power to the building. It is worth naming the layer in between, the one that decides how many accelerators exist before any of that matters — the package. CoWoS-L is in mass production at 5.5x reticle area, with 9.5x and 14x on TSMC's own roadmap, and at that size a 300mm wafer stops yielding accelerators in the dozens. Underneath sits a second, quieter dependency: the insulating film that makes the substrate work is about 95% one company's product, and it just got 30% more expensive.

TL;DR

A finished leading-edge wafer is not an accelerator. It becomes one only after CoWoS packaging bonds the compute dies and HBM stacks onto an interposer and a substrate — and that step is now the binding constraint. A reticle is about 26 x 33 mm, roughly 858 mm², the largest area a lithography tool exposes in one shot; CoWoS-S topped out at 3.3x reticle, and CoWoS-L moved to an RDL interposer with embedded silicon bridges to reach 5.5x (~4,719 mm²) in mass production in 2026, with 9.5x in 2027 and 14x in 2028 on TSMC's roadmap. On the July 16, 2026 earnings call, CEO C.C. Wei said packaging capacity is so tight that it is now limiting customers' growth. Estimated CoWoS capacity is ramping from roughly 75,000 to 125,000-130,000 wafers per month by end-2026 against estimated demand near 1 million wafers, with NVIDIA reportedly holding ~60% of it. And the substrate's key insulator, Ajinomoto Build-up Film, is ~95% supplied by one company, which raised prices about 30% from Q3 2026. Capacity numbers in wafers per month cannot be read as accelerator counts: at 5.5x reticle the largest packages yield single digits per wafer.

Every year the AI hardware conversation finds a new bottleneck and declares it the real one. First it was GPUs, then HBM, then substations, then water. The layer that keeps getting skipped sits between the wafer and the system: the package. It is where a working die stops being a die and becomes something you can bolt into a server, and in 2026 it is the step that rations everything downstream of it.

The reason is not mysterious, and it is not a shortage of cleverness. It is arithmetic plus a supply chain with a single point of failure in a place nobody outside the substrate industry looks.

What CoWoS actually does, in one paragraph

CoWoS — chip-on-wafer-on-substrate — takes the logic die (or dies), places HBM stacks around it on a silicon or redistribution-layer interposer that carries the dense wiring between them, then mounts that assembly on an organic substrate that talks to the server board. All three layers exist because the connections have to step down in precision: nanometre-scale on the die, micrometre-scale across the interposer, millimetre-scale on the board. Skip the middle layer and you cannot get HBM close enough to the compute to be worth the bandwidth.

The variants differ only in what the interposer is made of, and that difference is now the whole ballgame. CoWoS-S uses a full silicon interposer with through-silicon vias — the highest wiring density, in production since 2012, and hard-capped at 3.3x reticle area, about 2,700 mm², with room for up to 8 HBM stacks (four independent sources agree on the 3.3x ceiling). CoWoS-R swaps silicon for a polymer-and-copper redistribution layer: cheap, large, but too coarse for HBM-class links, so it lives in networking ASICs. CoWoS-L keeps the RDL interposer and embeds small silicon bridges — local silicon interconnect — exactly where the compute-to-HBM links need density. Silicon is used only where it earns its cost, which is what lets the package grow.

The reticle is the wall, and 5.5x is where we are

A lithography tool exposes at most one reticle field per shot: roughly 26 by 33 mm, or about 858 mm². No vendor prints a bigger die than that, so every large AI accelerator is a composition of reticle-limited dies inside a bigger package. The package size — how many reticles' worth of area you can assemble — is the number that decides how many HBM stacks fit, and therefore how much bandwidth per accelerator is even possible.

That number has been climbing. CoWoS-L entered volume at 3.5x reticle in 2024. TSMC's 2026 North America Technology Symposium put the current line at 5.5x reticle, about 4,719 mm², in mass production, with 9.5x in 2027 and a 14x package in 2028 described as capable of integrating roughly 10 large compute dies and 20 HBM stacks, followed by something beyond 14x in 2029. Trade coverage relaying the same roadmap quotes identical figures, and a 40x system-on-wafer part is the stated direction after that. Treat the post-2026 numbers as roadmap, not capacity: only the 5.5x figure is production.

The public proof that this is load-bearing is Blackwell. NVIDIA's own architecture disclosure describes two reticle-limited dies joined by a 10 TB/s die-to-die interconnect behaving as one GPU, with 208 billion transistors on a custom 4NP process; the Ultra variant carries 288 GB of HBM3e in eight 12-Hi stacks at 8 TB/s. That memory sits on the package. It is not a board DIMM, and it cannot be added later by a customer.

Why "wafers per month" is the wrong unit

Here is the arithmetic that makes packaging a wall rather than a queue. A 300mm wafer has about 70,686 mm² of area. At 4,719 mm² per 5.5x-reticle interposer, pure area division gives roughly 15 packages per wafer — before edge exclusion, test structures, or yield. Trade analysis of the largest CoWoS-class parts puts the real figure in the single digits per 300mm wafer. So when capacity is reported as "75,000 wafers per month," the accelerator count behind that number is not 75,000 of anything; it is a fraction of it that shrinks every generation as packages grow.

That is the mechanical reason a rising capacity figure does not translate proportionally into supply. It also explains why the roadmap itself is a cost story: to double output you either double wafers or double area per wafer, and the second path runs straight into yield.

What TSMC will and will not say

This forum has a rule about quoting vendors precisely, and packaging is where precision pays. On the July 16, 2026 earnings call, CEO C.C. Wei was asked about Intel's competing advanced packaging. His answer: TSMC's packaging capacity is so tight that it is now limiting its customers' growth — and he welcomed competitors taking on back-end load, because finished packages help sell TSMC's front-end wafers. That is a rationing statement from the only company that can currently supply the layer at scale, and it is corroborated across the call record and at least two independent trade write-ups.

What TSMC has not said matters just as much. It does not publish CoWoS capacity, and it has not called CoWoS "sold out" on its 2026 calls — one allocation tracker published an explicit correction to that effect in October 2026, after earlier versions of its own page described it as sold out. The widely repeated 52-to-78-week lead times are trade-report estimates, not vendor data. Q2 numbers are on the record: revenue US$40.2bn, gross margin 67.7%, capex guidance raised to US$60-64bn for 2026, of which advanced packaging, testing and masks sit inside a 10-20% bucket that management declined to split. When a company will not break out the line item that everyone says is the bottleneck, believe the direction and discount the decimal places.

The estimates worth carrying forward, all labelled as estimates: capacity rising from roughly 75,000 wafers per month (some trackers say 35,000 at end-2024) toward 125,000-130,000 by end-2026; demand growing from about 370,000 wafers in 2024 to 670,000 in 2025 and near 1 million in 2026; NVIDIA holding roughly 60%, about 595,000 wafers, with the top three customers above 85%. Those demand figures trace to a single sell-side house reproduced across outlets — which is exactly why they are stated here as one estimate with one origin, not as three agreeing sources.

The layer nobody prices: a film made by a MSG company

Under the interposer sits the substrate, and inside the substrate sits the insulator: Ajinomoto Build-up Film, a resin layer that insulates the microscopic wiring in high-end packages. It originated in the 1990s as a by-product of the company's MSG research, was industrialised with Intel in 1996, and has been close to a monopoly ever since. Current share is above 95%, with Sekisui Chemical holding most of the remainder — four independent sources agree, including the vendor's own disclosures as reported.

The numbers that make it a bottleneck rather than trivia: a high-end AI accelerator now uses 8 to 16 layers of the film, against 4 to 6 for a conventional PC chip — five to ten times the film per device, according to supply-chain estimates. Ajinomoto's capacity exceeded 2 million square metres per month in Q2 2026 and is running flat out. New capacity does not start building until 2028, and a third plant in Gifu is targeted for 2032. The company notified customers of a new pricing system from Q3 2026 with increases of up to 30% — the first such move in about a year — and the film's lead time has stretched beyond six months.

The demand gap is where the story gets uncomfortable. Estimates relayed from sell-side research put the global ABF substrate supply-demand gap at ~10% in H2 2026, widening to 21% in 2027 and 42% in 2028. A 30% film price rise on a material that is 25-30% of substrate BOM works out to a 7.5-9% substrate cost increase, of which roughly 3-6% is expected to reach final substrate pricing. This is a different kind of constraint from CoWoS: packaging capacity can be fixed with capital and time. A near-monopoly on an irreplaceable input, protected by three decades of patents and a 3-5 year customer qualification cycle, cannot be engineered around on the same clock.

Why this shows up in your per-hour rate

If a single 300mm wafer yields single-digit flagship packages, then the honest unit of AI supply is not the wafer and not the GPU — it is the qualified package. Three consequences follow, and all three are visible in rental markets.

First, supply grows slower than the headlines. Capacity announcements in wafers per month flatter the picture; the accelerator count behind them depends on package area, which is growing faster than yield improves. A market that expects 2x capacity to become 2x accelerators will keep being surprised by tightness.

Second, the mix is rationed, not allocated. With NVIDIA reportedly near 60% and the top three above 85%, smaller designers and internal cloud silicon teams compete for the remainder. That is why "we have the chips" claims from smaller providers deserve a follow-up question: which package, qualified when, at what yield.

Third, the constraint has no fast substitute. Intel's EMIB-T is a genuine architectural alternative — bridges in the substrate rather than a full interposer — and TSMC's CEO welcomed it out loud, which tells you how tight the back end is. But an alternative architecture is not shippable capacity until customers qualify it, and qualification is measured in quarters, not weeks.

For buyers of compute, the practical translation is unglamorous: per-GPU-hour prices will not fall as fast as wafer-capacity charts imply, because the expensive step is the one that turns wafers into products. And the supply that does come online tends to arrive as dense, single-vendor packages — which is precisely the case for assembling capacity from smaller, already-powered sites instead of waiting in a packaging queue (omc.network).

The 2026 reality check

State the soft spots plainly. The 5.5x-reticle production figure and the reticle dimension are solid — four sources, including TSMC's own symposium material as relayed by trade press. The 9.5x and 14x numbers are roadmap, and secondary sources already disagree at the margin (one table lists 9x for 2027), so treat anything past 2026 as direction. Capacity, demand, share and lead-time figures are all estimates that trace to a small number of sell-side houses; TSMC publishes none of them, and has explicitly not used the phrase "sold out" that half the coverage attributes to it. The ABF numbers are the best-corroborated block here — share, price increase and plant timing appear across four outlets — but the 10/21/42% gap is a research forecast, not a measurement. What survives all of that caveating is the structure: packaging is the step that converts wafers into sellable accelerators, it is rationed, its unit economics worsen as packages grow, and one of its key materials has a single supplier. Re-check the current numbers before you price anything. Do not expect the layer to get less important.

Key takeaways
  • A wafer is not an accelerator. CoWoS bonds compute dies and HBM onto an interposer and substrate; without that step a finished 3nm wafer is inventory, not product. It is the current binding constraint.
  • The reticle is the wall. One exposure covers ~26 x 33 mm (~858 mm²). CoWoS-S capped at 3.3x reticle (~2,700 mm²) with 8 HBM stacks; CoWoS-L reached 5.5x (~4,719 mm²) in mass production in 2026, with 9.5x (2027) and 14x (2028) on the roadmap.
  • Single-digit packages per wafer. Area division alone suggests ~15 packages per 300mm wafer at 5.5x; real yields for the largest parts fall to single digits — so wafers-per-month capacity cannot be read as accelerator counts.
  • TSMC's own words are a rationing statement. On the July 16, 2026 call, C.C. Wei said packaging capacity is "so tight that now it's limiting my customers' growth." TSMC publishes no CoWoS capacity and has not called it sold out; the 52-78 week lead times are trade estimates.
  • Concentration is extreme. Estimates put NVIDIA near 60% of 2026 CoWoS (~595,000 wafers) and the top three customers above 85%; demand is estimated near 1 million wafers against capacity of 125,000-130,000 per month.
  • The quiet single point of failure is a film. Ajinomoto Build-up Film is ~95% one supplier, AI accelerators use 8-16 layers of it, and prices rose up to 30% from Q3 2026 with no new plant before 2028 (third plant targeted 2032).
