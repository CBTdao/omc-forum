The utilization tax: most AI compute you rent runs at 30–50%, and that's the bottleneck nobody counts (2026)

This forum has spent a year locating the AI bottleneck — GPUs, then HBM, then power, then the building, then the package, then the water. The one that sits inside every one of those is quieter and more lucrative: most accelerators are paid for at 100% and used at a fraction of it. That gap is the utilization tax, and it is where the real, bookable supply is hiding.

DCF EditorialOct 26, 202611 min read
TL;DR

A data-center GPU is billed as if it were working every second. It rarely is. Public large-training disclosures put hardware utilization — model FLOPs utilization, MFU — in the 30–50% band: Google's PaLM reported roughly 46–47% MFU on TPUs, and GPU clusters commonly land lower, in the mid-30s to low-40s; datacenter-wide average GPU utilization measured by fleet telemetry has been reported in the 30–40% range across 2023–2025. That is not "broken chips" — the tax is collected in three structural places: (1) bursty, reservation-based workloads leave capacity idle between jobs; (2) the math is memory-bound, so ALUs wait on HBM — decode runs near 1 FLOP per byte against a balance point near 200; (3) scale-up overhead (all-reduce, checkpointing, pipeline bubbles) grows with cluster size, so bigger runs lose more to coordination. The arithmetic is brutal: a fleet billed at 100% but running at 40% MFU delivers 40% of its theoretical tokens. So when people say "there aren't enough GPUs," the precise claim is "there isn't enough used GPU-time" — and unlike a fab, that is fixable this quarter. It is also the Decentralized Compute thesis in one line: the world is short of utilized accelerators far more than it is short of accelerators, and the inventory that already exists — idle GPUs in offices, gaming rigs, workstations, small miners — is supply nobody has booked yet. (Disclosure: OMC runs a decentralized GPU network; the utilization point holds on the numbers regardless.)

This forum has a habit of finding the AI bottleneck one layer at a time. First GPUs, then HBM, then substations, then the building, then the package, then the water at the power plant. Every one of those is real. But they share a quiet common denominator that none of them names: a chip that exists, is powered, and is paid for is still not the same thing as a chip that is doing work. The gap between the two is the cheapest, largest, most ignored supply of AI compute there is.

This piece does the same thing the others did: separate two numbers that get quoted as one, find where the difference actually comes from, and work out what it does to the price you pay.

What "utilization" actually measures — and why two numbers get confused

The word gets used for at least three different things, and mixing them is how a 2× error enters a capacity plan. GPU utilization (often the SM-occupancy or DCGM metric) is the share of the chip's execution units that are busy at a sampled instant — commonly reported in the 50–70% range for actively running jobs. Model FLOPs utilization (MFU) is the harder number: actual floating-point ops delivered divided by the chip's theoretical peak, over a whole training run. Because of memory-bound phases and overhead, MFU is always far below the SM-occupancy number, and it is the one that decides your token throughput. Then there is slot occupancy — what fraction of reserved capacity is even running a job at all, before you ask how hard the chip inside is working.

A cluster can show 65% SM occupancy, 40% MFU, and 55% slot occupancy at the same time, and all three are true. Quoting the 65% as "how utilized we are" is the single most common way utilization gets oversold. The number that matters for supply is the product of all three, and it lands well under half.

Three places the tax is collected

The first is burstiness. Training and inference are not smooth rivers; they are waves. A reserved cluster sits idle between experiments, between checkpoints, between a batch finishing and the next one being staged. Reservation-based billing means you pay for the wave even during the trough. This is the largest, most struct­ural leak, and it is pure scheduling — not physics.

The second is memory-bound math. A modern accelerator does on the order of 1014 FLOP/s but moves bytes at a fraction of that. Decode — generating one token at a time — touches memory roughly once per FLOP, so it runs near ~1 FLOP per byte against a hardware balance point near ~200. The arithmetic units are literally waiting on HBM for most of every millisecond. This is the same wall the HBM deep-dive described, now expressed as idle ALUs rather than as a line item.

The third is scale-up overhead. The moment you train across hundreds or thousands of chips, you pay a coordination tax: all-reduce synchronizations, pipeline bubbles from imperfect micro-batching, and checkpoint stalls. Multiple published scaling studies show per-device efficiency falling as cluster size grows, because the communication and bubble cost grows faster than the extra compute helps. Bigger is not proportionally better, and the gap is paid in utilization.

Why the headline "shortage" is mislabeled

When an executive says "we cannot get enough GPUs," translate it carefully. A fleet billed at 100% but delivering 40% MFU is, for the purpose of tokens produced, a fleet that is 60% absent. Saying "we need more GPUs" when 60% of the existing ones are not producing is like saying "we need a bigger warehouse" when half the floor is empty. The constraint is used GPU-time, and used GPU-time is a function of software and scheduling, not of silicon.

This is why the same model that "needs" 25,000 H100s on one accounting can often be served with fewer, better-utilized devices on another. The difference is not the model. It is the tax.

What this means for the price you pay

Rent is quoted in $/GPU-hour, but value is delivered in $/token or $/finished run. The two diverge exactly by the utilization tax. A 40% MFU cluster charges you for 100% of the silicon and gives you 40% of the math; your effective $/token is 2.5× the theoretical floor. Two providers quoting the same $/hour can be 2× apart in $/token depending on how they schedule, and almost none of them print the MFU on the invoice.

For buyers, the practical move is unglamorous: ask for the utilization, not the rate. A lower $/hour on a 30%-MFU reservation can be a worse deal than a higher $/hour on a 50%-MFU one. The discount is in the utilization, and the utilization is the number nobody volunteers.

The distributed inventory nobody has booked

Here is where the editorial position of this site becomes relevant, and we state it plainly as a disclosure rather than sneaking it in. A decentralized GPU network's entire premise is that the shortage is one of coordination, not of hardware: there are millions of capable GPUs sitting in workstations, offices, gaming rigs and small mining rigs, used a few hours a day and idle the rest. Booking that idle time — already powered, already amortized, already cooled — is supply that requires no new fab, no new substation, no new packaging line. It does not replace frontier training clusters; it is the obvious place for the bursty, latency-tolerant, throughput-hungry middle of the market that is currently paying the full utilization tax on reserved hyperscale capacity.

The catch is real and worth naming: distributed supply is heterogeneous, harder to schedule, and higher-latency than a co-resident cluster. Pretending otherwise would be the kind of claim this forum exists to puncture. The point is narrower and stronger — the utilization tax proves there is far more usable compute than the "GPU shortage" headline implies, and the cheapest megawatts of AI capacity to unlock are the ones already humming in someone's office.

The 2026 reality check

State the soft spots. The single most important one: MFU and "GPU utilization" are different metrics and are routinely quoted as if they were the same, so any utilization claim needs the exact definition attached. Fleet-telemetry samples vary by who is measuring, what workloads are in the mix, and whether idle-but-reserved time counts as "used"; the 30–40% datacenter-wide figures trace to a small number of telemetry vendors and should be read as estimates, not measurements. The PaLM ~46–47% MFU is a real disclosure but it is TPUs and one model family, so it anchors the top of the band rather than defining it. What survives all the caveating is the structure, and it is robust: utilization is structurally far below 100%, the gap is large (often more than half), it is collected in predictable places, and it is the cheapest supply to unlock because it needs scheduling and software, not concrete. Re-check the current figures before you price anything, and when a provider quotes utilization, ask which of the three definitions they mean.

Key takeaways
  • Three different "utilizations." SM occupancy (50–70% on live jobs), model FLOPs utilization / MFU (the one that sets token throughput, 30–50% on big runs), and slot occupancy (is the reserved capacity even running a job). Quoting the first as "how utilized we are" is the most common oversell.
  • The tax is collected in three places. Bursty reservation-based scheduling leaves capacity idle between jobs; memory-bound math (decode ~1 FLOP/byte vs a ~200 balance point) leaves ALUs waiting on HBM; and scale-up overhead (all-reduce, checkpoint, pipeline bubbles) grows with cluster size, so bigger runs lose more.
  • 100% billed ≠ 100% delivered. A fleet at 40% MFU produces 40% of its theoretical tokens. "Not enough GPUs" is more precisely "not enough used GPU-time" — fixable with software and scheduling, not a new fab.
  • $/hour hides $/token. Rent is quoted per GPU-hour but value is delivered per token; the two diverge by exactly the utilization tax. A 40%-MFU cluster's effective $/token is ~2.5× the theoretical floor, and the MFU rarely appears on the invoice.
  • The cheapest supply to unlock is already humming. Idle GPUs in offices, workstations, gaming rigs and small miners are already powered, amortized and cooled — bookable without a fab, substation or packaging line. The hard parts are heterogeneity, scheduling and latency, not hardware.
  • Caveat the numbers. MFU vs GPU-utilization gets conflated; fleet telemetry varies by vendor and workload mix; the ~46–47% PaLM figure is TPUs/one family and anchors the top of the band. The robust claim: utilization is structurally well below 100%, often by more than half.
