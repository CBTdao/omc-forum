Big Tech's $4.49 trillion that is not on the balance sheet (2026)

On 2026-11-04, JPMorgan Asset Management published a chart that counts $4.49 trillion of AI-related lease and purchase commitments outside the seven biggest AI companies' balance sheets, against $758 billion inside them. That is 5.9 times as much. The commitments are disclosed in filings, not hidden — and that is exactly why the number deserves a careful reading rather than a slogan.

DCF EditorialNov 28, 202612 min read
TL;DR

Off-balance-sheet AI commitments are disclosed future contracts, not secret debt — and the scale is now the story. In Eye on the Market dated 2026-11-04, Michael Cembalest of JPMorgan Asset Management printed a chart sourced to Bloomberg, company filings and JPMAM (November 2026): across Microsoft, Alphabet/Google, Meta, Amazon, Oracle, NVIDIA and Anthropic, leases not yet started plus purchase commitments total $4.49 trillion, while long-term debt plus existing lease liabilities total $758 billion. JPMorgan's own text says only 15% of debt and lease commitments appear on balance sheet for the big seven. Our arithmetic: $4.49T ÷ $758B ≈ 5.9×, and $758B ÷ $5.248T ≈ 14.4%, consistent with the stated 15%. Anthropic's ratio is $865B ÷ $8B ≈ 108×; Alphabet's is $1.077T ÷ $113B ≈ 9.5×. Three independent relays (Catenaa, the Kobeissi Letter as summarized by Advance Intelligence, and Xinhua's 2026-11-22 wire) reproduce the same totals. Our read, stated as analysis: the risk is not that the obligations are invisible — it is that revenue has to arrive on schedule while the contracts are already fixed, and credit markets began pricing that timing before the debt line ever appeared.

中文摘要 · 标题：科技巨头那 4.49 万亿美元，并不在资产负债表上（2026）。摘要：摩根大通资产管理 2026-11-04 的《Eye on the Market》给出一张图表（数据源：Bloomberg、公司披露、JPMAM，2026 年 11 月）：微软、谷歌、Meta、亚马逊、甲骨文、英伟达、Anthropic 七家公司「尚未起租的租赁 + 采购承诺」合计 4.49 万亿美元，而「长期债务 + 现有租赁负债」合计仅 7580 亿美元；报告原文称七家公司「只有 15% 的债务与租赁承诺进入资产负债表」。我们的算术：表外是表内的 5.9 倍，表内占全部承诺的 14.4%，与 15% 一致；Anthropic 的表外/表内比为 8650 亿 / 80 亿 ≈ 108 倍，谷歌为 1.077 万亿 / 1130 亿 ≈ 9.5 倍。Catenaa、Kobeissi Letter（Advance Intelligence 转述）、新华社 11-22 电稿三家独立转述给出相同总额。分析判断（属我们的观点）：风险不在「看不见」——这些承诺都在财报里披露——而在于合同已锁死、收入必须按时到达；信用市场在债务尚未入表之前，就已经开始给这个时间差定价。

The one-sentence version: as of 2026-11-04, seven AI companies carried $4.49 trillion of leases not yet begun and purchase commitments outside their balance sheets against $758 billion of debt and lease liabilities inside them — disclosed, auditable, and 5.9 times larger than the line everybody reads.

This post belongs to the capital-chain series. The chain so far runs from supply commitments to public markets to the buyer's cash flow: NVIDIA's $390B of supply commitments (2026-11-21), Anthropic's $81.3B IPO against roughly $900B of compute commitments (2026-11-22), the four hyperscalers' $207B capex quarter (2026-11-23), and CoreWeave's $846M interest bill against $207M of operating income (2026-11-26). This installment steps one level up and asks what happens when the commitments sit outside the reported debt line. The answer requires precision about accounting, because the phrase "hidden debt" is wrong in a way that matters.

What exactly did JPMorgan count on November 4?

The primary artifact is specific: Eye on the Market, dated 2026-11-04, by Michael Cembalest, Chairman of Market and Investment Strategy at JPMorgan Asset & Wealth Management. The note says: "only 15% of debt and lease commitments appear on balance sheet for the big seven AI companies; that may explain why their credit spreads have been widening rapidly." Its chart is titled "Some things you can't see: $4 trillion in AI off balance sheet obligations, US$, billions" and is sourced to "Bloomberg, Company filings, JPMAM, November 2026." There is a small inconsistency worth naming: the chart's headline rounds to $4 trillion, but the bars add to $4,490 billion. Xinhua's 2026-11-22 wire uses the bar total, $4.49 trillion, and so do we — while noting that the chart label is a rounding, not a different dataset.

The two categories being added are leases that have not yet started and purchase commitments. The on-balance-sheet side is long-term debt plus existing lease liabilities. These are not the same thing as bank loans. A purchase commitment is a contract to buy chips, power or capacity in a future period; a lease not yet commenced is a signed obligation that has not been recorded as a lease liability yet. Both are normally disclosed in the notes to financial statements. Calling them "hidden debt" is therefore wrong twice: they are disclosed, and they are not debt instruments. Calling them "nothing" is also wrong, because they are contractual claims on future cash flow and the counterparty will expect payment.

Three independent relays reproduce the same figures. Catenaa (2026-11-08) reports approximately $4.5 trillion and prints the same per-company split. The Kobeissi Letter, summarized by Advance Intelligence on 2026-11-09, reports $4.49 trillion off balance sheet against $758 billion on it. Xinhua (2026-11-22) reports the JPMorgan November analysis as $4.49 trillion against roughly $758 billion and describes the former as nearly six times the latter. Four descriptions of one chart — primary plus three relays — agree on the arithmetic; where they differ is only in rounding language.

How unequal is the gap company by company?

Here is the chart as printed, with the ratio calculated by us. All figures are US$ billions as of November 2026; the ratio divides off-balance-sheet commitments by on-balance-sheet debt plus lease liabilities.

  • Alphabet / Google: $1,077B off balance sheet against $113B on it — 9.5× (ours). The largest absolute commitment of the seven.
  • Anthropic: $865B against $8B — 108× (ours). The smallest recorded debt line and the second-largest commitment, which is what happens when a private company finances a buildout through contracts rather than bonds.
  • Meta: $810B against $110B — 7.4× (ours).
  • Microsoft: $575B against $110B — 5.2× (ours).
  • NVIDIA: $422B against $37B — 11.4× (ours). The supplier's own commitments mirror the customers' purchase commitments.
  • Amazon: $419B against $223B — 1.9× (ours), the narrowest gap in the group.
  • Oracle: $322B against $157B — 2.1× (ours).

The sum is $4,490B + $758B = $5,248B ≈ $5.25 trillion of combined contractual obligations (ours), which is the figure the Kobeissi relay also prints. Our read, stated as analysis: the ranking by ratio is not a ranking by solvency. Amazon's 1.9× and Oracle's 2.1× can be produced by very different cash-flow profiles, and a low ratio on a weak cash flow is worse than a high ratio on a strong one. JPMorgan's own 2027 outlook notes that most of these companies' net-debt-to-EBITDA ratios remain below the S&P 500 median of about 1.9×, with Oracle an outlier near 4.2×; that is a different measurement from the commitment ratio and should not be mixed with it.

Why did credit spreads move before the commitments became debt?

Because credit markets price claims, not accounting line items. In the same note, JPMorgan's five-year credit-default-swap chart shows spreads widening across a set of AI-linked names through 2026, with Oracle near 300 basis points around October 2026; Catenaa's independent relay gives the same 300 bp level. JPMorgan attributes part of the recent equity selloff to hedge-fund profit-taking: gross long-short exposure fell 8% from its recent peak, the largest drawdown since 2016, sourced to Morgan Stanley Prime Brokerage data as of 2026-10-30. An independent English relay of the same research repeats the 8% figure.

Our reading, stated as analysis: the spread move is a rational response to timing risk. A debt instrument has a coupon date and a maturity; a $1.08 trillion pile of future leases and purchase commitments has a schedule that depends on data centers being finished, power being connected, and customers buying the output. A lender cannot see the final number on the balance sheet, but can see the contracts in the notes and the capital spending in the cash-flow statement. The market is discounting the gap between "contractually owed" and "operationally payable" — which is exactly the gap this post is about.

What does the 2028 permitting layer add to the arithmetic?

The same JPMorgan note adds a supply-side constraint that changes when those commitments can turn into revenue. It puts 37 GW of data-center capacity expected to be delivered in 2027, of which 75% is already under construction; and 96 GW expected in 2028, of which 37.5 GW may face a pause and another 12–15 GW may face conditions such as grid-cost payment, water limits and onsite backup power requirements. Two independent relays confirm the core figures: an English summary of the November 4 note gives 37 GW / 75% / 96 GW / 37.5 GW, and a Chinese energy-industry report gives 96 GW with 37.5 GW in pause jurisdictions. We are describing published institutional estimates of regulatory exposure, not taking a position on any policy.

There is a counterweight, and it belongs in the same paragraph as the headline. Bernstein, using the Aterio project-tracking database, probability-weighted 586 GW of announced North American projects down to about 199 GW realistically deliverable (34%); development stage accounted for roughly 198 GW of the reduction, tenant credit 113 GW, developer track record 48 GW, and state-level political risk only 28 GW — the smallest of the four haircuts. Two independent relays print the same breakdown. Our reading: the permit number is the visible risk because it arrives with a date; the development-stage number is the larger risk because it arrives with a spreadsheet. Both push in the same direction for the commitments above — a contract signed for 2028 capacity is not the same asset as 2028 capacity that has been energized.

What revenue is supposed to pay for all of it?

JPMorgan Private Bank's 2027 outlook, The Price of Progress, supplies the demand-side denominator. Its assumptions: about $40 billion to build one gigawatt of AI data-center capacity, roughly $11 billion of annual revenue per gigawatt to cover operating costs and capital, about 129 GW of frontier capacity by 2029, and therefore about $1.5 trillion of annual computing revenue — which, after the margins required by model and application companies, implies roughly $3 trillion of annual end-customer AI spending. Its illustrative potential is $3.57 trillion against a break-even of $2.87 trillion: a cushion of about $700 billion, explicitly conditional on adoption broadening beyond the technology sector. The figures appear on JPMorgan's own outlook page and are reproduced independently by the Financial Express and The Tribune (ANI).

Put the two JPMorgan artifacts side by side and the structure is clear. One counts $5.25 trillion of contractual obligations already signed by seven companies; the other says the entire ecosystem needs about $3 trillion a year of customer spending to justify the capacity those obligations are building. Those numbers are not commensurable — one is a stock of contracted cash flows spread over many years, the other an annual flow — and we will not pretend to subtract them. What they share is a dependency: the commitments are fixed in nominal dollars and the revenue is not. That asymmetry, not the word "hidden," is the thing to watch.

What would change this reading?

Three observable tests. First, the filings. If the next quarterly reports show the off-balance-sheet total falling while on-balance-sheet debt rises by a comparable amount, the transition is happening on the terms the market already understands and the spread premium should compress; if the total keeps rising without the debt line moving, the timing risk grows. Second, the completion rate. Watch the 2027 delivery cohort against the 75%-under-construction claim and the 2028 cohort against the pause/conditions estimates — a project that is paused still has its contracts, but not its revenue. Third, the adoption denominator. JPMorgan's own outlook flags that 1% of businesses account for 80% of AI spending; if that concentration does not broaden, the $3 trillion annual revenue line stays an illustration rather than a forecast. All three are checkable from public filings and published research, which is the standard this forum applies to every capital-chain post.

Key takeaways
  • The primary source: JPMorgan Asset Management, Eye on the Market, 2026-11-04, by Michael Cembalest — chart sourced to Bloomberg, company filings and JPMAM, November 2026.
  • The headline numbers: $4.49 trillion of leases not yet started plus purchase commitments, against $758 billion of long-term debt plus lease liabilities across seven companies; JPMorgan's text says only 15% of debt and lease commitments are on balance sheet.
  • Our arithmetic: off-balance-sheet is 5.9× on-balance-sheet; on-balance-sheet is 14.4% of the $5.248 trillion combined total; Anthropic 108×, Alphabet 9.5×, NVIDIA 11.4×, Meta 7.4×, Microsoft 5.2×, Oracle 2.1×, Amazon 1.9×.
  • The correction to the slogan: these obligations are disclosed in financial-statement notes and are not debt instruments — "hidden debt" is wrong; the risk is fixed commitments against uncertain revenue timing.
  • Credit market evidence: JPMorgan's five-year CDS chart puts Oracle near 300 bp in October 2026 (Catenaa agrees); gross long-short hedge-fund exposure fell 8% from its peak, the largest drawdown since 2016.
  • The supply constraint: 37 GW due in 2027 (75% under construction) versus 96 GW expected in 2028, of which 37.5 GW may be paused and 12–15 GW conditioned; Bernstein's probability weighting cuts 586 GW announced to 199 GW buildable, with political risk the smallest haircut.
  • The demand denominator: JPMorgan Private Bank's 2027 outlook requires roughly $3 trillion of annual end-customer AI spending against illustrative potential of $3.57 trillion — a conditional cushion, not a forecast.
