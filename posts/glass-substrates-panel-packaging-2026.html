From wafers to panels: the glass substrate pivot, and who ships first (2026)

Advanced packaging has been round for fifty years because wafers are round. The quiet pivot of 2026 is that the industry's largest players have all decided rectangles — large glass panels — are where the next doubling of package size lives. Intel demonstrated a working glass test chip in 2023 and claims a 10× interconnect density path. TSMC released a Glass Substrate Development for CoWoS plan to its supply chain in June 2026, testing 0.8mm glass cores in 85×110mm five-reticle packages. Absolics runs the first dedicated plant, built on a NIST-administered $75M federal award. This is a stage-by-stage read of who has actually published what — and what a buyer should and should not conclude from it.

DCF EditorialOct 27, 202611 min read
TL;DR

The substrate is the last layer of the packaging stack, and organic (ABF) substrates are the layer that limits how big an AI package can get — warpage and flatness degrade as packages grow, which is the problem our CoWoS piece ended on. Glass is the proposed replacement for the substrate core: Intel's September 2023 demonstration claims 10× interconnect density, 50% less pattern distortion, and support for packages up to 24×24 cm, targeting a trillion transistors on a package by 2030. In June 2026 TSMC issued its first public glass plan to the supply chain — partnering with Ibiden and Innolux, testing a 0.8mm glass core at 5× reticle (85×110mm) with no severe warpage or delamination, and reporting versus organic: package warpage improved 16%, effective CTE down 19%, stiffness up 31%, power resistance down 27%, inductance down 42%. The TrendForce-sourced roadmap — 2026 equipment/material validation, 2027 pilot, 2H 2028 mass production for panel-level packaging (CoPoS), pure glass-core substrates 2030+ — is an analyst estimate, not a TSMC commitment. Absolics (SKC) is furthest on the merchant side: a Covington, Georgia plant under a CHIPS award of up to $75M, with first customer deliveries expected 2025 and capacity from 2027. Honest summary: the physics case is real and now has three independent industrial bets behind it; the volume timeline is 2027–2030 and everything before that is samples, pilots, and validation.

Three posts ago this series established that the binding constraint on AI compute had moved from chips to packages: reticle limits, then the ABF film under them. This post picks up exactly where that one ended, because the industry did too. When the packaging wall binds, the response is not to optimize the wall — it is to change the material the wall is made of. In 2026, all at once and after a decade of silence, three independent players put glass on the record.

The reason glass keeps coming up is a short list of physics properties, and every serious writeup agrees on it. Glass is dimensionally stable where organic laminate shrinks and warps; it is flat at a level organic packages cannot hold, which matters because lithography focus depth is set by flatness; it tolerates higher temperatures; and its stiffness lets a package carry far more silicon before it bends. Intel's announcement is the most quantified version of the claim, and it has held up as the reference numbers ever since.

What Intel actually claimed in 2023 — and what it means

Intel's September 2023 demonstration — a fully functional glass-substrate test chip, built on roughly a decade of internal research and more than a billion dollars of R&D line in Arizona — put four numbers on the table that trade press and an academic review have repeated consistently since: up to 10× higher interconnect density than today's organic substrates, 50% less pattern distortion (which is what preserves lithography fidelity on a big, fine-pitch package), ultra-low flatness for better focus depth, and thermal/mechanical stability that permits much larger assemblies. Intel sized the opportunity in package terms: 50% more silicon per package, with so-called "system-in-packages" as large as 24×24 cm, and a roadmap framing of one trillion transistors on a package by 2030. Shipping was framed as "later this decade," with data-center and AI parts first.

Read those claims as what they are: a materials and dimensional argument, not a speed claim. Glass does not make transistors faster; it lets the package — the thing our CoWoS post showed is the actual rationing layer — keep growing when organic laminate gives up. That is why the numbers that matter downstream are dimensional: package area, interposer area, packages per wafer or per panel.

The 2026 status board: who has published what

TSMC is the most consequential entry, because it already owns the packaging bottleneck. On June 16, 2026 it released a "Glass Substrate Development for CoWoS" plan to its supply chain — its first public technical disclosure on glass — working with Ibiden (the ABF substrate incumbent) and Innolux (a panel-level display manufacturer). The published test data: a 0.8mm glass core in a 5× reticle CoW configuration measuring 85×110mm — large-AI-GPU class — with no severe warpage or delamination observed. Against organic substrates, the disclosed test comparisons were package warpage improved 16%, effective coefficient of thermal expansion down 19%, effective stiffness up 31%, and on power integrity, resistance down 27% and inductance down 42%. The strategic logic is panel-level packaging (branded CoPoS): move from round 300mm wafers to rectangular 310×310mm panels, where square/rectangular packages waste far less area at the edges.

Absolics, SKC's US subsidiary, is the furthest along the merchant path and — unusually for this industry — has a government paper trail anyone can read. The Commerce Department's CHIPS Program Office page records up to $75M in direct funding for a 120,000 sq ft facility in Covington, Georgia, with $343M in expected capital expenditure, first customer deliveries expected 2025 and production capacity from 2027. Korean trade reporting (ETNews, via multiple aggregators) puts the plant at ~12,000 m²/year and describes customer qualification discussions with AMD and AWS; treat both as reported rather than confirmed — the capacity figure and the customer list are single-upstream-sourced. At CES 2025 the company demonstrated glass-substrate packaging for AI data-center use with vendor claims (package thickness halved, faster data processing) that we note as marketing, not measurement.

The rest of the board, briefly: Rapidus (Japan) is developing square glass substrates for its 2nm chiplet strategy — Nikkei-reported dimensions of 30–100% more usable area and ~10× via density, again single-upstream. Samsung Electro-Mechanics operates a pilot line producing samples, with reported mass-production timing of 2027 or later. Ibiden — the company that benefits most from the ABF status quo — has glass-core R&D in its own 2023–2027 capex plan, which tells you the incumbent is not betting its existence on the status quo. And on the display side, BOE and Corning signed a three-year memorandum in May 2026 covering glass-based packaging substrates, one more sign that panel-manufacturing know-how is migrating from displays into packaging.

The arithmetic: what 85×110mm means

Now do the dimensional math, because it connects directly to the previous post. Our CoWoS piece showed that a 5.5×-reticle CoWoS-L interposer (~4,719 mm²) yields roughly 15 packages per 300mm wafer on paper, and single digits once real yields bite. TSMC's published glass test vehicle is a 5× reticle package at 85×110mm ≈ 9,350 mm² of package area. A 300mm wafer offers ~70,686 mm², so a round wafer fits at most 7 of these packages even with zero edge loss — and the corners a square package cannot use push that lower. On a 310×310mm panel (96,100 mm²), the same package fits 10 with materially less corner waste, and — the real point — the panel format is not the ceiling: the whole pitch of glass is that panels can keep getting bigger in a way round wafers cannot. (This division is ours, from the published areas; the published per-wafer yield figures in our previous post already showed how far paper area is from shipped units.)

The power-integrity numbers deserve their own sentence, because they are the least hyped and possibly the most consequential. Delivering hundreds of amps into a GPU package with minimal voltage droop is a substrate problem as much as a regulator problem, and TSMC's disclosed 27% lower resistance and 42% lower inductance versus organic is exactly the improvement that makes co-packaged power and higher current density practical. Signal integrity benefits the same way — lower loss at high frequency is why the optical-integration crowd co-sponsors glass research. Warpage gets the headlines; power delivery may be the bigger engineering unlock.

What could still kill it

The failure mode is not physics, it is yield economics, and every credible analyst writeup converges on the same list. Glass is brittle: drilling tens of thousands of micron-scale through-glass vias (TGV) without micro-cracks, metallizing them reliably, and keeping large panels intact through high-temperature processing are all harder than the equivalents on organic laminate. One Chinese brokerage research note circulating in trade coverage put the bar plainly: panel-level yield needs to stabilize above roughly 95% for costs to work — and no participant has published a yield number yet. That silence is informative. Intel's test chip was a demonstration, not a product; TSMC's June 2026 data is a validation experiment with partners, not a production commitment; and the widely-circulated 2026-validate/2027-pilot/2028-mass-produce sequence for CoPoS is TrendForce's reconstruction, not a TSMC press release. One careful analysis of TSMC's own comments noted that nothing in them commits a specific glass-core structure to production within any fixed window — TSMC is running a CoPoS pilot line and exploring substrates in parallel, which is what a company says when it is keeping options open.

There is also an obvious incumbency problem, and it is the mirror of the ABF story from our last post: Ajinomoto's film and the existing substrate makers do not disappear because a better material exists. The transition path that matters is hybrid — organic cores with glass interposers first, glass cores later — because that is the only ramp that reuses $billions of installed ABF capacity. Expect the marketing timeline and the deployment timeline to differ by years, exactly as CoWoS-L's did.

Why a compute buyer should care

Because every one of this series' bottlenecks — HBM stacks, reticle limits, the ABF film, transformer lead times, water — eventually turns into the same two numbers: how many accelerators can be built per year, and what each GPU-hour costs. Glass substrates are the industry's declared answer to the package-size half of that constraint. If the 2027 pilot and 2028 ramp land anywhere near the analyst timeline, the practical effects are: larger reticle-limit packages (more HBM stacks per accelerator, feeding directly into the HBM constraint from our earlier post), better power delivery per package, and — via panel economics — some relief on the packaging cost line that currently rations supply. If they slip, the 2026–2028 window stays CoWoS-bound and our packaging-wall post remains the accurate model.

The skeptical posture this forum takes on timelines applies fully here. Do not price 2028 panel capacity into anything today. Do read the June 2026 TSMC supply-chain plan as a signal that the largest foundry considers glass validation worth naming publicly — foundries do not publish partner validation programs for materials they have decided against. And note who is NOT waiting: Absolics is qualifying customers now, on US soil, with federal money behind the buildout. Distributed capacity models have a modest stake in this too — packaging cost and package-size ceilings set the economics of every compute node, whichever network it belongs to (omc.network).

The 2026 reality check

State the sourcing honestly. The Intel numbers — 10× interconnect density, 50% pattern distortion reduction, 24×24cm packages, 1T transistors by 2030, the decade of research and the Arizona line — come from Intel's own September 2023 announcement, reproduced consistently by PCMag, Chinese trade press, and a 2025 peer-reviewed review of glass substrates; they are claims by the party with the most to gain, not independent measurements. The TSMC June 2026 plan, its Ibiden/Innolux partnerships, the 0.8mm/5×-reticle/85×110mm test parameters and the 16/19/31/27/42 percent comparisons are consistently reported across TrendForce-sourced trade coverage (Securities Times, Ab-Semiconductor, and supply-chain recaps), but the underlying document went to TSMC's supply chain, not to the press — we are reading secondhand reports of a partner document. The 2026/2027/2H2028/2030+ roadmap is TrendForce's estimate and should be quoted as such. The Absolics facts that are solid — the $75M award, the facility, the capex, the delivery timeline — come from the NIST CHIPS Program Office page, a primary government source; the 12,000 m²/year capacity, the AMD/AWS qualification talks, and all Rapidus and Samsung Electro-Mechanics figures are single-upstream trade reporting, included here only with that label. The 95% yield bar is one brokerage's view. No yield figures from any participant were published as of this writing, and every production date in this piece is an estimate or a plan, not a booked schedule.

What survives the caveating is structural and unlikely to move: organic substrates are the size-limiting layer, glass is the only material with a quantified dimensional case to replace them, three independent industrial players have publicly staked 2026–2030 timelines on it, and the first volume-relevant milestones land in 2027. Everything else is a plan that can slip. Watch for exactly two signals in 2027: a TSMC pilot-line announcement with a named product, and an Absolics capacity figure confirmed by its customers. Everything before that is samples.

Key takeaways
  • The substrate is the last un-rationalized layer. HBM, reticles and the ABF film were the earlier walls; organic substrate cores are the layer that limits package size next — warpage and flatness degrade as packages grow.
  • Intel's 2023 claim is still the reference numbers. 10× interconnect density, 50% less pattern distortion, 50% more silicon per package, packages up to 24×24 cm, 1T transistors on a package by 2030 — a dimensional argument, not a speed claim.
  • TSMC went public in June 2026. A supply-chain "Glass Substrate Development for CoWoS" plan with Ibiden and Innolux: 0.8mm glass core, 5× reticle, 85×110mm, no severe warpage; warpage −16%, CTE −19%, stiffness +31%, power resistance −27%, inductance −42% versus organic.
  • The roadmap is an estimate, not a commitment. 2026 validation → 2027 pilot → 2H 2028 CoPoS mass production → 2030+ pure glass cores is TrendForce's reconstruction. TSMC has committed to no specific glass structure or date.
  • Absolics has the hardest paper trail. NIST's CHIPS page: up to $75M, 120,000 sq ft in Covington GA, $343M capex, first deliveries 2025, capacity 2027 — the only player whose plant facts are a government primary source.
  • The dimensional math favors panels. A 9,350 mm² package fits ~7 per 300mm wafer but ~10 per 310×310mm panel, with less corner waste — and panels, unlike wafers, can keep growing.
