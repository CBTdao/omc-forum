A 1.73% quarter, a 1.41% lifetime: reading Backblaze's Q2 2026 drive stats like a homelab buyer

Backblaze's Q2 2026 Drive Stats report landed on September 29, 2026: 354,415 hard drives analyzed, 1,498 failures, a quarterly annualized failure rate of 1.73% — the highest in quite a while, up from 1.24% the quarter before. The lifetime number barely moved: 1.41%. Both facts are the same fact, and the gap between them is exactly the reading skill that separates a smart used-drive purchase from an expensive one.

DCF EditorialNov 20, 202610 min read
TL;DR

AFR is a fraction, and the denominator is where the information lives. In Q2 2026 (April 1–June 30), Backblaze monitored 359,101 drives, removed boot drives and models that didn't meet criteria, and analyzed 354,415 of them over 31,553,350 drive days with 1,498 failures — a quarterly AFR of 1.73%, the highest in recent quarters (Q1 2026 was 1.24%). The lifetime table — 353,041 drives, 558,148,607 drive days, 21,609 failures — sits at 1.41%, a slight uptick but essentially flat. The quarter's three outliers tell you why the gap exists: a 12TB HGST at 7.63% (nearly seven years old), a 10TB Seagate at 9.33% (about 8.5 years old, only 965 units, 22 failures) and a 14TB Seagate at 8.26% (1,235 units, 25 failures). Meanwhile 10 of 31 models exceeded 3.0% AFR — a skew Backblaze itself flags as unusual. And the zero-failure "honor roll" includes a model with all of 1,499 drive days of exposure — about 6 days per drive. Nothing about that is durability evidence. For a homelab buyer paying doubled prices for capacity: compute AFR yourself as failures ÷ drive-days × 365, read the denominator first, weight age over brand, and treat any model's small-sample quarterly number — good or bad — as noise until lifetime-scale drive days exist.

The question this piece answers: Backblaze just published its highest quarterly drive-failure rate in years — should a homelab buyer care? Short answer: not about the headline number. This piece walks the Q2 2026 report (published September 29, 2026, verified against Backblaze's own post and independent transcriptions on 2026-11-20), shows why the quarterly and lifetime numbers diverging is the real story, and extracts the screening method that survives any market price. It connects to two earlier pieces in this section: the SSD endurance method post (DWPD/TBW — the flash side) and the used-GPU TCO post (the compute side). This one is the spinning-rust side.

What did the Q2 2026 report actually say?

The census: 359,101 drives monitored during the quarter; Backblaze removed 3,881 boot drives and 705 others that didn't meet inclusion criteria, leaving 354,415 drives for analysis. The quarter: 31,553,350 drive days and 1,498 failures, for a quarterly AFR of 1.73% — which the report itself calls "the highest it's been in quite a while," up from 1.24% in Q1 2026. The lifetime ledger: 353,041 drives, 558,148,607 drive days, 21,609 total failures, 1.41% — described as a slight uptick in a number that has been stable for years.

AFR itself is one line of arithmetic: failures ÷ drive days × 365. Drive days exist because models have different population sizes and different exposure windows; annualizing normalizes them into "what share of drives would fail in a year." The formula is trivial. The skill is in remembering that every AFR is a fraction — and asking what the denominator actually contains before reacting to the numerator.

Why did the quarterly number spike while the lifetime number didn't move?

Because a handful of old, small cohorts dragged the average, and the report says so with its own quartile analysis. Three models were statistical outliers (defined as AFR above 6.95%): a 12TB HGST HUH721212ALN604 at 7.63%, nearly seven years old; a 10TB Seagate ST10000NM0086 at 9.33%, about 8.5 years old with only 965 units in service; and a 14TB Seagate ST14000NM0138 at 8.26% from a population of 1,235 averaging five and a half years old. AFRs that high on old, thin populations are the signature of aging hardware, not of a fleet-wide problem — which is exactly why the lifetime number, dominated by 558 million drive days of mixed-age exposure, registered barely a tremor at 1.41%.

Two more quarter-specific facts sharpen the point. First, 10 of 31 reported models exceeded 3.0% AFR — a distribution skew Backblaze explicitly flags as unusual and "something to keep an eye on." Second, the quarter's failure table is also where drives go to die of old age: two models — a 4TB HGST at nearly nine years and an 8TB HGST at nearly eight — were fully retired this quarter. The spike is partly just the fleet graying: capacity-era cohorts bought in bulk years ago are entering the steep part of the mortality curve, and their small surviving populations make their AFRs volatile.

Why is the zero-failure honor roll a trap?

Because zero failures proves nothing without exposure. This quarter's honor roll: Seagate ST8000NM000A (8TB), ST12000NM000J (12TB) and ST14000NM000J (14TB) with zero failures, and ST16000NM000J (16TB) with one. Look at the denominators, though. The ST8000NM000A: 239 drives, 1,499 drive days — about 62 days of average exposure per drive, with an average age of roughly half a month. A brand-new cohort running for two months without a failure is a statement about the factory escape rate, not about five-year durability. (Backblaze knows this — those same three models fail its own lifetime-table inclusion minimums, precisely because their drive counts and drive days are too small to say anything.) We think the honor roll is best read as an operations report — new deployments, concentrated in 20TB+ capacity, no new drive models for two consecutive quarters — not as a shopping recommendation.

The inverse trap is just as real on the bad side: that 14TB Seagate's 8.26% came from 25 failures across 110,410 drive days. Terrifying percentage, modest absolute event count, tiny denominator. Compare the workhorse Seagate ST16000NM001G: 35,100 units, 3.16 million drive days in the quarter, 56 failures, 0.65% — and 0.68% lifetime over 47.2 million drive days. Same fleet, same quarter; the difference between 8.26% and 0.65% is mostly the denominator.

What should a homelab buyer actually do with this?

Four screening habits, all of them price-independent — which matters in a market where popular NAS capacities have roughly doubled and the used 14TB tier repriced with them. First, read the denominator before the percentage. Any AFR quoted without drive days is a rumor; Backblaze's own inclusion criteria (over 100 drives and 10,000 drive days for a quarterly table, over 500 and 100,000 for lifetime) are a reasonable minimum standard to demand of anyone else's failure claims too. Second, weight age over brand. The quarter's three outliers span two manufacturers; what they share is 5.5–8.5 years of age. The lifetime table's 0.5–1.2% models are spread across HGST, Seagate, Toshiba and WDC — no vendor monopoly on survival. Third, use the shape of the curve, not just the level. Drive mortality concentrates early (infancy failures) and late (wear-out), which is why Backblaze's multi-year curves have shown elevated first-year and aged-fleet rates since its earliest reports — and why a used drive that has already survived its first year is, statistically, in the calm part of the bathtub. Fourth, screen SMART before price. The attributes that have historically carried signal in fleet-scale analyses are the reallocation cluster — SMART 5 (reallocated sectors), 197 (pending sectors), 198 (offline uncorrectable) — plus error-rate counters; a used drive with any nonzero pending or uncorrectable sectors is a drive you rebuild around, not one you deploy. None of this is a substitute for backups; it is how you avoid buying the rebuild in the first place.

One last calibration for scale. A 1.41% lifetime AFR means a single drive is roughly a coin-flip to survive eight years unfailed — probability of survival ≈ (1 − 0.0141)^8 ≈ 89% (our arithmetic). That is why the practical unit of homelab reliability is never the drive; it is the redundancy scheme around the drives, plus the restore you have actually tested. The Q2 2026 report's real headline for a homelab reader is not "drives got worse." It is: at 354,415 drives, failures are ordinary, aging is visible, and every dramatic percentage is hiding a denominator that explains it.

Key takeaways
  • Q2 2026 in one line: 354,415 drives, 31,553,350 drive days, 1,498 failures, quarterly AFR 1.73% (highest in a while, up from 1.24% in Q1) — while the lifetime AFR stayed at 1.41% over 558,148,607 drive days.
  • The outliers explain the spike: 7.63%, 9.33% and 8.26% AFR — all on drives 5.5–8.5 years old and/or in populations under 1,300 units. 10 of 31 models exceeded 3.0%, a skew Backblaze itself flags as unusual.
  • The zero-failure honor roll is an exposure statement, not a durability claim — one honored model had only 1,499 drive days (~62 days per drive), and the same three models miss Backblaze's own lifetime-table minimums.
  • Read the denominator first: 8.26% AFR on 110,410 drive days (25 failures) vs 0.68% lifetime on 47.2 million drive days — same fleet, same quarter, different denominators.
  • Age beats brand: the quarter's worst drives span two vendors; the best lifetime performers span four. Two models retired this quarter at 8–9 years — that's the failure mode to plan around.
  • Survival arithmetic (ours): (1 − 0.0141)^8 ≈ 89% — a single drive is a coin flip over eight years. The unit of reliability is the redundancy scheme plus a tested restore, never the drive.
