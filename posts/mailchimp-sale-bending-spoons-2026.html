Intuit sold Mailchimp for less than a third of what it paid: the contact-list business got repriced (2026)

On 2026-11-20, Intuit agreed to sell Mailchimp — the most recognizable brand in email marketing — to Bending Spoons at an enterprise value of about $3.68 billion. Intuit paid roughly $12 billion for it in November 2021. The write-down is 69%, and the business behind it grew 0% while the AI buildout around it grew triple digits. This post reads the deal number by number, because the gap between $12B and $3.68B is the cleanest public data point on what happened to standalone subscription software during the two years AI ate the growth budget.

DCF EditorialNov 27, 202612 min read
TL;DR

Mailchimp sold for 2.8× revenue; it was bought for a growth story that never showed up. Per the announcement on 2026-11-20, confirmed independently by four outlets citing both companies' statements: enterprise value ≈ $3.68B — $3.31B in cash plus $368M in Bending Spoons stock — against the ~$12B Intuit paid in November 2021, a 69% write-down. The business case is printed in Intuit's own results: Mailchimp revenue was $1.3B in the fiscal year ended July 2026, flat since 2024, and $313M in Q1 FY27 (−1% YoY) — inside an Intuit quarter that grew 12% to $4.35B. Deal terms: expected close early 2027, subject to regulatory approval; Intuit routes the cash to FY27 buybacks and cut FY27 revenue guidance to $23.081–23.365B (growth 8–9%, from 9–10%) on Credit Karma softness, while keeping profit guidance. The buyer, Bending Spoons — a Milan operator whose stated model is acquiring mature software brands (AOL, Vimeo, Evernote, WeTransfer, Eventbrite) and running them "in their forever home" — reported Q3 2026 revenue of $780M (+125%) and holds Berkshire Hathaway as a ~4% shareholder. Our arithmetic: at $1.3B of revenue, the sale price is 2.8× FY26 revenue; the $8.3B of value Intuit destroyed equals 6.4 years of Mailchimp's entire revenue; and Mailchimp's flat line against the AI infrastructure complex's triple-digit growth is the demand-side answer to this forum's supply-side posts — the money for GPUs has to come from someone's software budget, and email marketing stopped being the budget line that grows.

The one-sentence version: five years after Intuit paid roughly $12 billion for Mailchimp, it agreed on 2026-11-20 to sell the same asset for about $3.68 billion — a 69% write-down on a business whose revenue has been flat since 2024 — and the price gap says more about where software budgets went than any earnings call this quarter.

This post belongs to the forum's capital-chain series but sits at a different layer than usual. The earlier posts followed the supply side: NVIDIA's $390B of supply commitments (2026-11-21), Anthropic's $81.3B IPO against ~$900B of compute commitments (2026-11-22), the four hyperscalers' $207B capex quarter (2026-11-23), and CoreWeave's $846M interest bill (2026-11-26). This one follows a dollar in the opposite direction — out of a software category that stopped growing. Email marketing is where mid-market companies spent their "reach more customers" budget; when generative AI tools started absorbing that budget line, the category's price tag moved. The Mailchimp sale is the receipt.

What was actually announced on November 20?

The structure, per both companies' statements as relayed by four independent outlets (Zhitong Finance via multiple syndications, Sina Finance's global markets desk, Guandian, and IT Times): Intuit agreed to sell Mailchimp to Bending Spoons at an enterprise value of approximately $3.68 billion, composed of $3.31 billion in cash and $368 million in Bending Spoons stock. Intuit's proceeds come with a six-month contractual lock on the shares it receives, and the company said it plans to use the cash for share repurchases in fiscal 2027. The transaction is expected to close in early 2027, subject to regulatory approval and customary conditions. Intuit CEO Sasan Goodarzi framed the sale as focus and capital allocation — concentrating on QuickBooks, TurboTax and Credit Karma, the products being layered with AI agents — and Intuit simultaneously reported first-quarter results and trimmed guidance.

One detail in the buyer's disclosure is easy to skip and worth not skipping: the same week's reports note Bending Spoons received a Berkshire Hathaway investment of roughly 4% of the company — Zhitong's breakdown: a new position of about 20.7 million shares worth about $689M built during Q3, topped up with $330M on October 14; Sina's relay cites a disclosed ~$930M. The two relays differ at the "approximately" level but agree on the stake and the timing, so we print the stake (about 4%) and flag the cash figure as relay-dependent. A value-oriented buyer of record behind an aggregator of mature software brands is itself a statement about what this asset class is now understood to be: cash flow, not growth.

What does the revenue line say?

Everything the valuation needs. Mailchimp generated $1.3 billion of revenue in the fiscal year ended July 2026, a figure that has been essentially flat since 2024. In Intuit's first fiscal quarter of 2027 (ended 2026-10-31, reported 2026-11-19/20), Mailchimp revenue was $313 million, down 1% year over year — annualized, about $1.25B, i.e. the flat line has started tilting down. Put the sale price on that base: $3.68B ÷ $1.3B ≈ 2.8× revenue (our arithmetic). For comparison, Intuit itself grew total Q1 revenue 12% to $4.35B with adjusted EPS of $2.91 against a $2.70 consensus — the seller is healthy; the asset simply stopped participating in the growth.

The same earnings call quantified what the sale does to guidance. Intuit cut FY27 revenue guidance to $23.081–23.365 billion — growth of 8–9% instead of the prior 9–10% — attributing the trim to Credit Karma, not to the divestiture (guidance excludes Mailchimp's pending sale effects, updated at close). Second-quarter guidance of $4.93–4.95 billion came in below the ~$5.11B consensus, and the stock fell sharply pre-market on the combination. Profit guidance was maintained. The read: Mailchimp was worth more to Intuit as balance-sheet capacity for buybacks and AI investment than as a business line, and management priced that preference publicly.

How did $12 billion become $3.68 billion?

In November 2021, at the top of the zero-rate SaaS repricing, Intuit bought Mailchimp for approximately $12 billion to fold email marketing into QuickBooks' SMB stack. Our arithmetic on the write-down: ($12B − $3.68B) ÷ $12B ≈ 69%. The $8.3B of value destroyed equals about 6.4 years of Mailchimp's entire current revenue — a decade-scale mispricing compressed into five years. No single disclosure explains the gap, but the pieces are visible in the two companies' own numbers. On Intuit's side, the 2021 thesis was cross-sell into a customer base QuickBooks already owned; a flat top line since 2024 is the market's verdict on how much of that cross-sell actually happened. On the category side, Mailchimp's own published pricing tells the defensive story: the free plan's contact limit has been cut repeatedly (2,000 → 500 → 250 per an independent price timeline), list prices rose (Standard $14.99 → $20), and the flagship AI assistant ships as a free beta add-on — features bundled to defend a seat, not priced to grow.

The buyer's model explains the rest. Bending Spoons describes itself on its own site as an acquirer that makes acquired products "their forever home" — it does not re-sell; it operates for cash. Its portfolio (AOL, Vimeo, Evernote, WeTransfer, Eventbrite, Meetup, Brightcove, StreamYard, plus 2026 acquisitions of Miro at ~$1.36B EV and Airtable at ~$1.29B EV per Zhitong) is a deliberate assembly of mature subscription brands. Its own reported Q3 2026: revenue $780M, +125%; adjusted operating income $424M, +130%; net debt $4.0B, leverage 2.1×. In other words, the buyer paid 2.8× revenue for Mailchimp out of a model that buys flat-cash-flow software cheaply and harvests it. That is what the asset is worth in 2026's market: not a growth multiple, a cash-flow multiple.

Why is this a compute-forum story and not just a software M&A story?

Because the flat line and the AI buildout are the same phenomenon viewed from opposite ends. Every post in this series has traced money toward GPUs: NVIDIA booking $115.8B of quarterly revenue with $390B of supply commitments; Anthropic raising $107.3B against ~$900B of compute obligations; the hyperscalers spending $207B in one quarter; CoreWeave paying $846M a quarter in interest to own capacity early. All of that money comes from someone's budget. In 2023–2025, a meaningful slice came from the marketing-and-growth line of small and mid-sized businesses — the exact line email marketing platforms compete for. When AI tools began writing campaign copy, generating audience segments, and answering customers directly, that line did not disappear; it moved from per-seat subscriptions to usage-priced AI. Mailchimp's response — bundling Intuit Assist free, raising list prices on the seat — is the pricing posture of a category defending margin on a shrinking clock.

The buyer side completes the picture. Bending Spoons runs its own AI infrastructure layer — its published stack includes GPU task balancing for inference across its products — which makes it a consumer of exactly the capacity this forum's supply-side posts describe, acquired at asset prices rather than at growth prices. The deal is a data point for a claim we have made before and mark again as analysis: the AI buildout is funded partly by repricing every software category that AI makes commoditized, and the repriced cash flows then buy the GPUs. Mailchimp's 69% write-down is what one link in that chain looks like when it is large enough to require a press release.

What should a Mailchimp customer or operator actually do?

Nothing dramatic, and that is the practical point. The deal closes in early 2027 at the earliest, and both companies committed to continuity; an acquisition by an operator whose model is long-term ownership is, if anything, a stability signal compared with a strategic buyer who might sunset the product. What the deal does change is expectations: pricing discipline at acquired subscription brands tends to tighten (the operating model depends on it), so audit your contact count — Mailchimp bills every contact in the audience, not just the reachable ones — and treat any annual prepayment discount as a decision that should wait for the new owner's first pricing move. Our view, marked as a view: the more durable lesson is for anyone selling software to SMBs in 2026. The 2021 pricing of that market — 15× forward revenue for a seat-based growth story — is gone; the 2026 clearing price for a flat $1.3B asset is 2.8×. Whatever you are building, the multiple you are implicitly assuming is the one the market paid last week, not the one Intuit paid in 2021.

What would change this reading?

Three measurable things. One: the close itself. Regulatory approval is pending; if the deal breaks, Intuit retains a $1.3B-revenue asset it publicly tried to exit, and the strategic signal inverts. Two: the first Bending Spoons pricing move. If Mailchimp's list prices or contact-meter rules change within two quarters of close, that is the operating model asserting itself and the category's floor moves with it; if pricing holds for a year, the "acquirer squeezes" narrative is wrong for this deal. Three: the Q1-to-Q2 revenue slope. Mailchimp declined 1% in the September quarter. If the December quarter shows the decline accelerating past 3–4% before the close, the category-level read — AI absorbing the growth budget — is confirmed at the asset level; if it flattens, the write-down was a multiple story, not a demand story. Each of these is checkable from public filings and published pricing pages, which is the standard this forum holds itself to.

Key takeaways
  • The deal: announced 2026-11-20 — enterprise value ≈ $3.68B ($3.31B cash + $368M Bending Spoons stock) against ~$12B paid in November 2021: a 69% write-down; close expected early 2027 pending regulatory approval.
  • The business behind it: Mailchimp revenue $1.3B in FY26 (ended July 2026), flat since 2024; $313M in Q1 FY27, −1% YoY — while Intuit's total Q1 grew 12% to $4.35B with a beat on EPS.
  • The arithmetic: sale price ≈ 2.8× FY26 revenue; $8.3B of value destroyed ≈ 6.4 years of Mailchimp's entire revenue.
  • The buyer: Bending Spoons — long-term operator of mature software brands (AOL, Vimeo, Evernote, WeTransfer, Eventbrite; 2026: Miro ~$1.36B, Airtable ~$1.29B) — Q3 2026 revenue $780M (+125%), adj. operating income $424M (+130%), net debt $4.0B at 2.1× leverage, with Berkshire Hathaway holding ~4%.
  • The guidance context: Intuit cut FY27 revenue guidance to $23.081–23.365B (8–9% growth, from 9–10%) on Credit Karma, kept profit guidance, and routes sale proceeds to FY27 buybacks.
  • The structural read (analysis): the money that funds the AI buildout comes partly from repriced software categories; email marketing's flat line and the GPU complex's triple-digit growth are two ends of one budget shift.
