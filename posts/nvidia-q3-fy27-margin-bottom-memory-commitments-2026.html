NVIDIA's first $115.8B quarter: the margin guide is the real headline (2026)

On November 17, 2026, NVIDIA reported fiscal Q3 2027 (quarter ended October 25, 2026): revenue $115.8 billion (+103% year over year), data center revenue $107.6 billion (+110%), net income $65.3 billion. The market cheered the beat. The more honest number sits two lines lower: gross margin guided from 74.9% down to 71–72% next quarter — the company's own declared bottom — while $390 billion of supply commitments (mostly memory) and $118 billion of customer guarantees accumulate behind the growth.

DCF EditorialNov 21, 202612 min read
TL;DR

Profit is now bigger than revenue used to be. NVIDIA's Q3 FY2027 — reported November 17, 2026 for the quarter ended October 25 — booked $115.8B of revenue (+103% YoY, +20% QoQ), $107.6B from data center (+110%), GAAP net income of $65.3B and diluted EPS of $2.70. One line from the official income statement frames the scale: quarterly net income ($65.3B) now exceeds the company's total revenue four quarters earlier ($57.0B). But the release's real information content is the outlook: Q4 guided to $130.0B ±3% with gross margin of 71.0–72.0% — roughly three points below Q3 — which interim CEO/CFO Colette Kress called the bottom of the cycle, attributed to honoring previously agreed purchase-order prices while absorbing higher component costs, recovering to 72–73% in FY2028 under new pricing. Behind the growth, the balance sheet tells the financing story: supply and capacity commitments rose from $279B to $390B in one quarter (the increase primarily multiyear memory procurement), maximum guarantee exposure reached $118B (including $105B tied to a 20-year OpenAI-leased campus project in Ohio), $32B of conditional agreements with specialized AI clouds were disclosed, accounts receivable grew $18.1B in a single quarter, and inventory hit $39.7B for the Vera Rubin ramp — which contributed nearly $20B in its first quarter of production shipments. China contributed $2.4B of licensed Hopper revenue in Q3 and is guided to zero in Q4. And the quarter was the first led by a leadership team without co-founder Jensen Huang, who stepped down as president, CEO and director on November 12, 2026, with Kress serving as interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent successor.

The question this piece answers: NVIDIA just booked the most profitable quarter in corporate history — so why did management spend the call talking about a margin floor? Short answer: because the quarter's composition changed. This piece walks the Q3 FY2027 print (reported November 17, 2026; verified against NVIDIA's official press release and multiple independent transcriptions on 2026-11-21), separates the two revenue records from the one line that actually moved the story — the margin guide — and then reads the balance-sheet disclosures that most coverage skipped: $390B of supply commitments, $118B of guarantees, and a receivables line growing faster than revenue. It connects to three earlier pieces here: the DRAM long-term-agreement post (the memory suppliers' side of the same negotiation), the AI server builder cash post (the OEM layer below NVIDIA), and the hyperscaler depreciation post (the customers' books). This is the layer where all three meet.

What did the quarter actually print?

The official press release is unambiguous about the scale. Revenue of $115.844 billion — up 20% sequentially and 103% year over year — with data center revenue of $107.6 billion (+21% QoQ, +110% YoY) carrying about 93% of the company. GAAP gross margin printed 74.8% (non-GAAP 74.9%); GAAP operating income hit $77.5 billion; GAAP net income was $65.3 billion with diluted EPS of $2.70 (non-GAAP EPS $2.68). Free cash flow was $43.8 billion against operating cash flow of $48.3 billion. The company returned $28.1 billion to shareholders during the quarter — buybacks plus a $0.25 dividend — under a repurchase authorization that the board expanded by $150 billion in September 2026 without expiration, leaving roughly $227.3 billion unused at quarter end.

One comparison from the release's own tables does more work than any adjective: net income of $65.3 billion exceeds total revenue of $57.0 billion from the year-ago quarter (Q3 FY2026). In four quarters, the profit line outgrew the revenue line of the period everyone still called NVIDIA's blowout. That is the scale on which everything else in this quarter — including a 3-point margin concession worth real billions — has to be read.

Why is the margin guide the real headline?

Because it is the one number management volunteered as a problem. Q4 FY2027 is guided to $130.0 billion, plus or minus 3% — roughly 12% sequential growth, helped by an extra week in the fiscal quarter and zero assumed China data-center compute revenue. Gross margin is guided to 71.0–72.0%, GAAP and non-GAAP alike — about three points below Q3. On a $130B revenue base, each point of gross margin is roughly $1.3B per quarter, so the guided concession is on the order of $3.9B of gross profit per quarter at the midpoint (our arithmetic). Kress was explicit about both the cause and the floor, per the call as reported by multiple outlets: NVIDIA is honoring previously agreed purchase-order prices while absorbing higher component costs — the surge in memory pricing being the largest component of that absorption — and the repricing that restores profitability arrives with fiscal 2028, where margins are guided back to 72–73%. Her framing, as quoted in call coverage: "What we provided here in Q4 is our bottom."

That mechanism matters more than the percentage. NVIDIA signed supply commitments when component prices were lower and is delivering against customer POs priced earlier; the margin dip is the accounting shadow of a timing mismatch between two stacks of fixed-price contracts. The fix is not efficiency — it is repricing the next round of agreements on both sides. Analysts watching FY2028 should treat the 72–73% recovery guide as a claim about NVIDIA's pricing power, not about component costs: it requires that AI compute demand keep outrunning the memory bill, which is exactly the bet the DRAM suppliers made when they moved the market onto multi-year contracts (a shift we covered from the suppliers' side in the DRAM long-term-agreements piece). If memory prices keep climbing past the new pricing bands, the "bottom" gets re-tested; if demand softens, repricing gets harder. The margin guide is where NVIDIA's two hundred-billion-dollar market assumptions meet arithmetic.

What does $390 billion of supply commitments buy — and for whom?

The quarter's most consequential balance-sheet line got a line item, not a headline. Supply and capacity commitments — money promised to suppliers for processors, memory, substrates and packaging — rose from $279 billion at the end of Q2 to $390 billion at the end of Q3, an increase of $111 billion in thirteen weeks. Per the CFO commentary as reported by multiple independent outlets (English-language trade coverage and financial press in two languages agree), the increase was primarily multiyear memory procurement. Read against our DRAM post from November 15: the memory suppliers' long-term agreements that locked buyers into price bands and deposits have now shown up in the buyer's own filings — NVIDIA is the counterparty putting the "L" in LTA. The commitments exist to secure HBM and DRAM years ahead for a ramp that has already begun: Vera Rubin NVL72 shipped in Q3 in what the release calls the fastest product ramp in company history, running at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure and Nebius, with call coverage putting Rubin's first-quarter contribution at nearly $20 billion — roughly 18% of the quarter's data center revenue from a platform that began shipping mid-quarter.

Inventory tells the same story from the warehouse side: $39.7 billion at quarter end, up from $31.6 billion the previous quarter — components bought ahead for the ramp (the inventory build plus the receivables surge below also explain why operating cash flow of $48.3B trails net income of $65.3B). And the guarantee disclosures quantify how far NVIDIA now reaches into its customers' own financing problems. Maximum exposure under various guarantees reached $118 billion, up from $108.5 billion the prior quarter — including $105 billion of credit support tied to an Ohio AI-campus project that will serve OpenAI under 20-year leases, structured so the guarantees take effect only as buildings complete (first exposure not expected before fiscal 2029, per the CFO commentary as reported). Separately, $32 billion of agreements with specialized AI cloud providers were disclosed — deals where NVIDIA earns hardware revenue plus a share of cloud rental revenue above a guaranteed minimum, conditional on the customer securing its own financing and commissioning the facilities. Cloud-service and data-center-lease commitments, per the same call coverage, rose from $74B to $102 billion. One more line ties it together: accounts receivable grew $18.1 billion in a single quarter (from the cash-flow statement's change in receivables). NVIDIA is no longer just selling compute — it is underwriting the buildout that buys its compute. That is the same working-capital pattern we documented at the server-builder layer in the Dell/Supermicro cash post, now visible one level up the stack, at roughly ten times the scale.

What did the China line do — and why does Q4 assume zero?

Q3 included $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper (H200) data-center revenue from China, sold under the US-approved arrangement that had shipments routed through the United States subject to 25% tariffs. On the call, Kress said NVIDIA is no longer shipping licensed Hopper data-center products to China, and the Q4 guide embeds zero China data-center compute revenue. The release itself confirms the guidance assumption ("NVIDIA is not assuming any Data Center compute revenue from China in its outlook"). The 25% tariff framework was the previous leadership's signature arrangement, and its quiet end — not announced as policy, just absent from the guide — marks the second structural change of the quarter: NVIDIA's fastest-growing product line now runs entirely on demand NVIDIA does not need to share with a geopolitically constrained market. For capacity planners the arithmetic is direct: Q4's $130B midpoint is guided despite removing a revenue stream that contributed $2.4B in Q3, with the extra week and the Rubin ramp carrying the load.

Who ran the call — and what changed besides the numbers?

This was the first quarter reported without co-founder Jensen Huang in the executive chair. On November 12, 2026, the board announced that Huang had stepped down as president, CEO and director, effective immediately, after a personal-conduct disclosure the board said it investigated independently; chief financial officer Colette Kress was appointed interim chief executive while continuing as CFO, and board member Harvey C. Jones took the chair. The official release itself carries the change in a signature line: Kress is quoted as "interim CEO and executive vice president and chief financial officer." On the call, Jones said the board unanimously accepted the resignation and does not intend to reinstate Huang, with an external search under way for a permanent CEO; Huang, in a public statement the day before the print, said he would return if the board judged it in the company's interest. Coverage of the call was notably consistent on one stylistic point: where Huang's calls sold vision and product cadence, Kress's call spoke in commitments, conditions, fulfillment schedules and guarantee mechanics — the CFO's grammar for a company whose growth is now partly a financing operation. The board also established a standing National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee (announced the same week as the earnings), formalizing the compliance posture that the China guidance change reflects.

Two disclosures on the call deserve a flag for anyone modeling FY2028. First, the raised growth outlook — FY2028 revenue guided to grow about 75%, up from roughly 70% set the prior quarter — is explicitly supply-constrained: management framed it as what current and incremental supply can support, not what demand implies. Second, Kress signaled that support for neocloud customers will be "disciplined and selective," with future guarantees and investments rigorously evaluated — the first public softening of the financing posture that produced the $118B exposure figure. Both statements are risk-management language from an interim CEO whose credibility rests on the balance sheet, not the keynote. We think that is the right posture for the data: when supply commitments grow $111 billion in a quarter, the marginal question stops being "how fast can demand grow" and becomes "how priced-in is the memory curve."

What should a compute buyer take from this quarter?

Three habits transfer from this print to any infrastructure decision. First, read the guide's mechanics, not its midpoint. $130B ±3% is the headline; the embedded assumptions (an extra week, zero China, Rubin ramping) are where the model risk lives. A guide that absorbs a $2.4B revenue removal and still implies 12% sequential growth is a stronger demand statement than the beat itself. Second, trace the margin concession to its counterparty. A vendor absorbing component costs to honor old POs is a vendor that has pre-sold its pricing power; the recovery guide (72–73% in FY2028) is a claim you can hold them to, and the memory suppliers' own LTAs — with floor prices set at cycle highs, as our DRAM piece documented — are the reason the concession exists. Third, treat guarantee and commitment disclosures as forward capacity signals. $390B of supply commitments tells you component makers' order books are full into FY2028; $118B of customer guarantees tells you which buyers NVIDIA believes need credit support to consume that supply. Both numbers say the same thing from opposite ends: the constraint on AI compute growth in 2027 is no longer demand, and it is no longer only silicon — it is the financing stack that connects them.

Summary: in the quarter ended October 25, 2026, NVIDIA earned $65.3 billion of net income — more than its total revenue four quarters earlier ($57.0 billion) — while guiding gross margin down roughly three points to 71–72%, carrying $390 billion of supply commitments that grew primarily from multiyear memory procurement, and disclosing $118 billion of maximum guarantee exposure: the most profitable quarter in corporate history is also, in the most literal filing sense, a prepayment for next year's memory bill.
