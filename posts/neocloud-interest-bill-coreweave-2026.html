A neocloud does not earn its growth, it borrows it: CoreWeave's $846M interest bill against $207M of operating income (2026)

CoreWeave (Nasdaq: CRWV) is the cleanest read on how the AI build is financed outside the hyperscalers, because it has no other business to hide behind. In the third quarter of 2026 it reported $3.661 billion of revenue, $207 million of operating income — and $846 million of net interest expense. The backlog is $132.5 billion. The debt is $46.9 billion against $4.0 billion of equity. Both numbers can be true at once, and the gap between them is the whole story.

TL;DR

A neocloud converts debt into GPUs and GPUs into contracted revenue; the conversion rate is visible in the interest line. Per CoreWeave's Q3 2026 earnings release filed with the SEC on 2026-11-10: revenue $3.661B (+168% YoY), operating income $207M, net interest expense $846M, net loss $709M, adjusted EBITDA $2.256B, cash capital expenditure $9.441B, operating cash flow $1.975B, revenue backlog $132.5B. The arithmetic: operating income is 4.1× smaller than the interest bill; adjusted operating income of $379M covers 45% of interest; the company guides Q4 net interest to $1.05–1.15B against an implied Q4 adjusted operating income of roughly $0.8B (FY guide midpoint $1.325B minus $528M booked in nine months), i.e. 1.3–1.4× the interest bill. The backlog is about 10.1 years of FY26 revenue at the guidance midpoint, and it grew $28.5B in one quarter against $3.661B of revenue — a 7.8× book-to-bill. On the funding side: total debt $46.9B ($41.6B recourse, $5.3B non-recourse) against $4.0B of equity and $87.5B of total liabilities on $91.5B of assets; 2026 capex guidance of $37–39B is about 2.9× revenue. The credit market has already priced the marginal dollar: the 2032 unsecured notes issued at par in June 2026 at a 9.625% coupon traded near 78–80 cents in mid-October with a yield around 15%, five-year CDS peaked at 1,194.5 basis points (ICE Data Services, reported independently by four outlets) implying a >60% five-year default probability, and S&P rates the issuer B. Our reading: at a 15% marginal cost of capital, the full $46.9B debt stack would cost about $7.0B a year in interest — 5.3× the entire FY26 adjusted operating income guidance.

The one-sentence version: CoreWeave sold $3.661 billion of compute in Q3 2026 and paid $846 million to the people who lent it the money to build the capacity — so the operating business earned $207 million while the balance sheet charged $709 million of net loss. Everything else in this post is the detail behind those four numbers, and the detail is where the interesting questions live: how a $132.5 billion backlog can coexist with a 4% operating margin, why depreciation lags the interest bill, and what the bond market's 15% yield says about the price of the next gigawatt.

This is the fourth post in the forum's capital-chain series. The first three traced the same dollar from different seats: NVIDIA's Q3 FY27 order book and $390B of supply commitments (2026-11-21), Anthropic's $81.3B IPO against ~$900B of compute commitments (2026-11-22), and the four hyperscalers' $207B quarter with three of four free-cash-flow negative (2026-11-23). This one sits one layer down, at the leveraged buyer: the company that rents the GPUs it borrowed to buy.

What did CoreWeave actually report for Q3 2026?

Numbers below are from the earnings release attached as an exhibit to the Form 8-K filed 2026-11-10, for the quarter ended 2026-09-30, with the prior-year quarter for comparison. Revenue $3,661M versus $1,365M, a 168% increase. Operating income $207M (6% margin) versus $52M (4%). Net interest expense $846M versus $310M — a 173% increase, five points faster than revenue. Net loss $709M (−19% margin) versus $110M. On the non-GAAP lines the company prefers: adjusted EBITDA $2,256M (62% margin) and adjusted operating income $379M (10% margin), the latter down from a 16% margin a year earlier even as dollars almost doubled. Nine-month figures: revenue $8,314M, operating income $14M, net interest $2,022M, net loss $2,075M.

The cash-flow statement is where the scale shows. Operating activities produced $1,975M in the quarter; purchases of property and equipment consumed $9,441M, so free cash flow — not a figure the company reports, but the subtraction any reader can do — was −$7,466M. Over nine months: $5,638M of operating cash against $23,558M of capex, or −$17,920M. Financing activities supplied $7,361M in the quarter, including $10,301M of net debt issuance, partly offset by $2,344M of repayments and $566M paid for capped calls on the convertible notes. The balance sheet closed with $56,778M of net property and equipment (from $30,557M at 2025-12-31), $18,922M of operating lease right-of-use assets, $4,122M of cash, and $87,456M of total liabilities against $91,481M of assets — a 95.6% liabilities-to-assets ratio, with $4,025M of equity underneath.

Why is the interest line the real income statement?

Because it is the only line that scales with the assets the company has already bought, whether or not customers have started paying for them. Interest on $46.9 billion of debt accrues from the day the money is drawn. Revenue from a GPU cluster accrues from the day the cluster is delivered and the contract ramps. Between those two dates sits construction in progress, and the gap is visible in the depreciation line: $1,889M of depreciation and amortization in the quarter, which annualizes to roughly $7.6B against $56.8B of net PP&E — about 13.3%, below the ~16.7% that a six-year straight-line life would imply. Some of that PP&E is not yet serving customers, so it is not yet depreciating. Interest has no such grace period. That asymmetry is why operating income can grow 298% year over year (from $52M to $207M) while the company still reports a $709M quarterly loss: the operating line is catching up to an asset base the financing line is already charging for.

Three ratios frame it. Operating income is 4.1× smaller than net interest ($207M against $846M). Adjusted operating income covers 45% of it ($379M against $846M) — so even on the company's own preferred measure, the operating business funds less than half the cost of its capital. And the forward guide is starker: with $528M of adjusted operating income booked in nine months against a full-year guide of $1.275–1.375B, Q4's implied adjusted operating income is roughly $0.8B, while guided Q4 net interest is $1.05–1.15B — 1.3–1.4× the quarter's operating profit, before depreciation, before capex. The company discloses this guidance itself, which is the part worth respecting: management is not hiding the shape of the bill, it is telling you the bill arrives before the earnings do.

How big is a $132.5 billion backlog, really?

A backlog is a duration claim, not cash. CoreWeave defines revenue backlog as remaining performance obligations "plus other amounts we estimate will be recognized as revenue in future periods under committed customer contracts," subject to delivery and service availability — which is a definition that includes contracts that have not started, at prices that may have escalation clauses, on capacity that does not yet exist. Read against the revenue guide midpoint of $13.15B, $132.5B is about 10.1 years of revenue. That is not a number any single customer contract runs for; it is the arithmetic signature of a book that is growing faster than it is being delivered. The quarterly delta makes the point: backlog went from roughly $104B at the end of Q2 to $132.5B at the end of Q3 — +$28.5B in three months against $3.661B of revenue booked in the same three months, a 7.8× book-to-bill. For scale, a year earlier the backlog was about $55.6B.

Two other lines make the backlog legible. Deferred revenue — customer money already received for service not yet delivered — is $2,269M current plus $7,733M non-current, about $10.0B, or 21% of total debt: customers are prepaying a fifth of the financing. And the CEO's own guidance says roughly $9B of customer prepayments and deposits are expected in the next three months alone. A backlog of $132.5B tells you what has been promised; $10B of deferred revenue tells you what has been paid. The distance between the two is the part that still needs a lender.

Who is actually paying for the build?

Follow the guidance: 2026 capex of $37–39B against revenue of $13.0–13.3B — capex at about 2.9× revenue — with year-end active power above 1.85 GW and contracted power already above 4.6 GW, of which more than 1.3 GW is international. A company spending three dollars on assets for every dollar of revenue cannot fund the difference from operations; the $17.9B nine-month free-cash-flow deficit says so directly. So the funding stack, in the company's own disclosure: $4.2B raised through an upsized convertible senior notes offering; an inaugural at-the-market equity program for up to 35 million Class A shares; and the $2.6B DDTL 5.5 facility — described in the release as the company's "first financing facility designed to support shorter-duration customer contracts."

That last phrase is the most informative sentence in the release. A lender building a facility around shorter-duration contracts is a lender shortening its own tenor — pricing the mismatch between multi-year GPU assets and contracts that may not run as long as the depreciation schedule assumes. Check it against the balance sheet: $41.6B recourse debt and $5.3B non-recourse, which means most of the borrowing is secured against the parent rather than ring-fenced against a single project's cash flows. Equity of $4.0B against $46.9B of debt is 11.6× leverage, and the accumulated deficit has grown to $4,718M from $2,643M at the start of the year. When a capital-intensive company's equity is 4.4% of its assets, the asset values — GPU resale prices, contract enforceability, utilization — are not accounting footnotes. They are the collateral.

What does the credit market charge for the next dollar?

Less than the equity market's enthusiasm, and more than the income statement admits. CoreWeave issued 2032-maturity unsecured notes with a 9.625% coupon at par in June 2026. By mid-October those notes were quoted at roughly 78–80 cents, a yield around 15% (one relay puts it at 15.4%, another "approaching 15%"; the 2031 notes with a 9.75% coupon were quoted above 15.3%). The five-year credit default swap spread — the cost of insuring its debt against default, per ICE Data Services — peaked at 1,194.5 basis points, having widened 74.5 to 135 basis points in a single session depending on the relay, which implies a five-year default probability above 60%. S&P's issuer rating is B, four notches below investment grade. Four independent outlets (China Fund News, Deep Market, NextFin, Caijing) report the same figures from the same ICE data, which is why we are willing to use them; the price level itself we have not verified against a primary bond quote, and readers should treat the yield as an order of magnitude rather than a tick.

Price that against the earnings. At a 15% marginal cost, the full $46.9B debt stack would cost roughly $7.0B a year in interest — 5.3× the entire FY26 adjusted operating income guidance of $1.275–1.375B. The company's actual Q3 interest bill of $846M annualizes to $3.4B, roughly half the market-clearing number, because most of the debt was raised before spreads widened. That is the mechanical reason the refinancing calendar matters more than any single quarter: every maturity reprices from the 6–9% coupon era into a 15% market. The equity has already re-rated for it — the stock traded near $69.70 on 2026-10-15, about 54% below its 52-week high of $150.40, after a single-day drop of up to 16% when the bond story broke.

How is this different from the hyperscalers?

Not by exposure to AI demand — by who carries the financing. The four hyperscalers spent roughly $207B of capex in the September quarter and three of them ran negative free cash flow, but they funded it from a combination of operating cash flow, investment-grade bonds and, in Alphabet's case, an inventory build that consumed $11B of cash in nine months. Their cost of debt is a spread over Treasuries in the tens of basis points. CoreWeave's marginal cost is 15%, its rating is B, and its assets are GPUs whose residual value nobody has marked to market at scale. The same dollar of AI revenue therefore has a different shareholder behind it at each layer: at the top, an investment-grade balance sheet absorbing a temporary cash deficit; here, a leveraged lessor whose interest bill is 4.1× its operating income.

That is also why the two halves of this series should be read together. The hyperscalers' commitments are the demand side of the same contracts that fill CoreWeave's $132.5B backlog; NVIDIA's $390B of supply commitments are the upstream mirror. When one of those three layers reprices, the others feel it — and the layer with 11.6× leverage and a B rating is the one that feels it first.

What would change the conclusion?

Three things, all measurable, none of them rhetorical. One: utilization at scale. The company's own target is an annualized run-rate of $20–21B by year-end on more than 1.85 GW of active power — roughly $11B of revenue per gigawatt per year. If that lands and holds, revenue per unit of capex improves and the interest coverage arithmetic moves in the right direction. If contracts ramp slower than capacity is energized, the interest bill arrives first, as it did this quarter. Two: the tenor of new facilities. The DDTL 5.5 structure is explicitly built for shorter-duration contracts. If future facilities keep shortening, lenders are telling you the contract book is less durable than the asset life. If a long-dated, investment-grade-style facility appears, the opposite. Three: refinancing spreads. A 15% marginal cost applied to $46.9B is $7B a year; a return to 9% would be $4.2B. Nothing in the operating business changes when that spread moves — which is precisely why it is the variable to watch. Our view, marked as a view: this is not a fraud story or a bubble story, it is a duration story. The company has bought assets with 5–6 year lives using debt priced for the next refinancing window, against contracts whose ramp is not fully visible in the backlog number. That works as long as the capital markets keep rolling the paper and utilization keeps climbing. It is the same condition the hyperscalers face, at ten times the leverage and without an investment-grade floor.

Key takeaways
  • The interest line is the business model: Q3 2026 revenue $3.661B, operating income $207M, net interest expense $846M — operating income is 4.1× smaller than the cost of the capital that produced it.
  • Even the company's preferred metric covers less than half: adjusted operating income $379M = 45% of the interest bill; Q4 guided interest of $1.05–1.15B is 1.3–1.4× the quarter's implied adjusted operating income of ~$0.8B.
  • Depreciation lags; interest does not: D&A of $1,889M annualizes to ~13.3% of net PP&E, below a six-year life's ~16.7% — the gap is assets not yet in service, and only one of the two charges waits for delivery.
  • Backlog is duration, not cash: $132.5B ≈ 10.1 years of FY26 revenue at the guide midpoint, up $28.5B in a quarter against $3.661B of revenue (7.8× book-to-bill); deferred revenue of $10.0B is the part customers have actually paid.
  • Leverage is the collateral argument: $46.9B of debt ($41.6B recourse) against $4.0B of equity, on $91.5B of assets with $87.5B of liabilities — and a $2.6B facility built for "shorter-duration customer contracts."
  • The market prices the next dollar at ~15%: 2032 notes at a 9.625% coupon traded near 78–80 cents, five-year CDS peaked at 1,194.5bp (implied >60% default probability), rating B. At 15%, the full stack costs ~$7.0B a year — 5.3× FY26 adjusted operating income guidance.
